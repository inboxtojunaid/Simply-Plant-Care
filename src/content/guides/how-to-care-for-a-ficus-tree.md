You moved it three feet. Now the floor is covered in leaves.

That's a ficus — most often *Ficus benjamina*, the weeping fig — doing the thing it's famous for. Move it, water it differently, let a cold draught in, repot it, and leaves fall. It looks like a disaster. Usually, it isn't. Leaf drop is a complaint, not a death sentence, and a tree that still has supple branches and small green buds will almost always grow its leaves back once conditions settle.

This guide starts where you're actually standing: on a floor full of leaves, wondering what you did wrong. The routine comes second. Because if you don't know what triggered the drop, the routine is just guesswork.

## Why it dropped its leaves this week

Work backwards. What changed in the last two weeks? A ficus drops leaves in response to change, and the change is almost always one of five things: light, water, draughts, a move or repot, or pests. Most of the time it's the first four, and most of the time only one of them changed — find it, fix it, and stop fiddling.

A move across the room counts. So does a new curtain, darker autumn days, or a tree that spent a week in a different spot while you painted. Light changes are the quietest trigger because nothing looks different to you — the room looks the same, but the tree is getting two hours less sun than it was.

Watering changes are sneakier still. A week of extra watering because you felt guilty, or a fortnight of forgetting because you were busy — both cause leaf drop, and they look similar until you check the soil. Wet soil plus falling yellow leaves means too much water. Dry soil plus falling green leaves means too little. The soil check, not the calendar, tells you which one you're dealing with.

Cold draughts are the classic sudden cause: a frequently opened front door, a draughty window, an air-conditioning vent blowing across the canopy. Radiators are the opposite problem — hot, dry air that dries leaf edges and invites spider mites. A tree can sit happily for months and then drop leaves the week the heating comes on.

Repotting is the most reliable trigger of all. A ficus resents being repotted, and a repot plus a move plus new watering habits in the same week is three changes at once — the tree can't tell you which one it objected to, so it drops leaves at all three.

Then there's pests, the least common cause but the one to rule out before you relax: speckled leaves, fine webbing, sticky residue, or small brown bumps on the stems. If you see any of those, the drop has a different explanation and a different fix.

Fix the most likely cause, keep everything else steady, and give it several weeks. A ficus that keeps dropping leaves while you change its light, its water, its pot, and its position all in one weekend never gets the chance to tell you which fix worked.

## The first two weeks after a drop

So what do you actually do, today, with a half-bare tree? Less than you think.

First, check the branches. Bend a few gently. Supple and green inside when scratched lightly? Alive. Brittle and snapping dry? That branch is dead — prune it off. Small green buds along the stems? New leaves are already on the way, and they'll usually appear within a few weeks in the growing season.

Second, fix the cause you found above and nothing else. Moved it into a draught? Move it back. Overwatered? Let the soil dry out and check the drainage. Underwatered? Water thoroughly and drain well. Then stop. Don't feed it to "help it recover" — fertiliser on a stressed tree usually makes things worse, pushing soft growth the tree can't support. Don't repot it. Don't move it again to a "better" spot. Every further change is another thing for it to adjust to.

Third, sweep up the leaves and wait. This is the hardest part, because waiting feels like doing nothing. It's not. A ficus that has stopped dropping and started budding is recovering, and the recovery is measured in weeks, not days. If it's still dropping heavily after three or four weeks with steady care, re-check: is there a draught you missed, a radiator that just came on, or pests on the leaf undersides?

One thing that helps while you wait: a quarter turn every couple of weeks is fine. Shifting it across the room every week is not. The difference is scale — a small rotation keeps growth even without changing the light the tree receives; a new position is a new adjustment.

## The routine that stops the next drop

Once the tree has settled, the routine that keeps it settled is almost boring. That's the point. Ficus trees want consistency the way some people want routine — same spot, same rhythm, no surprises.

Light first. Bright, indirect light: near a south- or west-facing window but set back a little from the glass, or near a bright east-facing window. A few hours of gentle morning sun is fine; strong midday sun through glass can scorch the leaves. Low light is the slow killer here — leaves drop from the lower and inner branches first, growth turns sparse, and the tree thins out over months. If that's happening, move it closer to the window gradually, over a week, not in one jump.

Water second. The rhythm matters more than the amount on any single day. Check the top 3–5 cm of soil; water when it feels dry; soak thoroughly until water runs out of the drainage hole; empty the saucer after fifteen minutes. In spring and summer that may mean weekly watering; in winter, every two weeks or less. A tree in standing water drops leaves fast, so the saucer step isn't optional.

Temperature and humidity third, and they're really about neighbours. Ficus wants warm, steady room temperatures, roughly 15–25°C. What it doesn't want is a front door that opens onto it all day, or a radiator drying the air beside it. If your home is very dry in winter, group the tree with other plants or stand it on a pebble tray. A light misting helps the leaves briefly but doesn't change the room's humidity for long — don't rely on it alone.

Soil and feeding are the quiet parts of the routine. Ordinary houseplant potting mix with a handful of perlite, in a pot with drainage holes. Feed with a balanced liquid fertiliser at half strength, once a month, spring and summer only. Nothing in autumn and winter.

## Repotting and pruning without starting a new episode

Repot only when the tree is clearly root-bound — usually every two to three years in spring — and go up just one pot size. Fresh mix, same routine afterwards, and expect some leaf drop anyway. That's the tree adjusting, not failing. Keep conditions steady while it settles and don't change anything else that month.

Prune in late winter or early spring, before growth restarts. Remove dead or crossing branches, shorten overly long shoots to keep the shape, and thin the centre lightly so light reaches the inner leaves. Don't remove more than about a third of the canopy in one year. The sap is milky and can irritate skin, so wear gloves and wipe the secateurs clean afterwards. Pruning after a leaf-drop episode, once the tree has stabilised, encourages bushy regrowth — but prune a tree that's still actively dropping and you'll add a second stress on top of the first.

## Still dropping? Check these last

A bare bottom with leaves only at the top is common in low light or after years without pruning. Brighter spot, prune the top back in spring, and the lower branches get their chance.

Dry, crispy leaf edges point to dry air or underwatering — check the soil first, then look at the neighbours. A radiator or a hot, sunny window can dry leaves even when the soil is moist.

Sticky leaves or tiny bumps on the stems mean scale insects, leaving sticky honeydew. Wipe them off by hand, wash the foliage, and check nearby plants. Spider mites leave fine webbing and speckled leaves; a lukewarm shower and higher humidity help. Isolate the tree while you treat either one.

And if nothing has changed, nothing is wrong with the spot, the soil is right, and it's still dropping? Look up. A tree directly under a heating vent or in the path of an air-conditioning draught can drop leaves for months before anyone connects the two. The vent is easy to miss because it's not at plant height.

## Questions, answered briefly

### Why is my ficus dropping leaves?

Something changed — a move, a change in light, a cold draught, a watering change, or repotting. Work through the checklist above, fix the most likely cause, keep conditions steady, and give it several weeks to settle.

### Can it recover after losing most of its leaves?

Often, yes. If the branches are still flexible and there are small green buds, the tree is alive. Keep it in bright indirect light, water correctly, avoid moving it, and new leaves usually appear within a few weeks in the growing season.

### How often should I water it?

When the top 3–5 cm of soil feels dry, soaking thoroughly and letting the excess drain away. Roughly weekly in summer, every two weeks or less in winter, depending on light and temperature.

### Does it like direct sunlight?

A little gentle morning sun is fine, but strong midday sun through glass can scorch the leaves. Bright, indirect light is the safest choice indoors.

### When should I repot?

Every two to three years in spring, or when roots are circling the pot and growth has stalled. Go up one pot size, use fresh mix, and expect some leaf drop afterwards.

### Is ficus safe for pets?

*Ficus benjamina* is toxic to cats and dogs if eaten, and its milky sap can irritate skin. Keep it out of reach of pets that chew plants and wear gloves when pruning.
