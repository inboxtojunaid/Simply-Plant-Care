Ferns have no flowers. No seeds. They reproduce by spores, fine as dust, tucked under their fronds — and they have been doing it that way for hundreds of millions of years, long before flowering plants existed at all.

That history explains the whole job. Ferns come from forest floors, where the soil never fully dries out and the air is always damp. They do not want more sun. They do not want more food. They want moisture — in the soil, and in the air — and when they do not get it, they crisp at the edges, drop fronds, and take a long time to recover.

## The verdict in three lines

Keep the soil moist like a wrung-out sponge, never letting it dry out fully, and check it every two or three days.
Keep the air at 50% humidity or higher — group plants, use a pebble tray, or run a humidifier — because below 40% the fronds crisp.
Give it bright, indirect light, keep it away from radiators and direct sun, and feed it weakly, only in spring and summer.

That is the whole guide, in three lines. The rest is what those lines look like in a real house, in winter, when the heating is on and the air is dry.

## How damp is damp enough? Reading your room

Most homes are too dry for ferns, especially in winter when the heating is on. Humidity below 40% will crisp the edges of the fronds. Ferns want 50% or more.

How do you know what your home is? A cheap hygrometer — a small digital humidity meter, a few pounds — tells you in seconds, and it is worth having if you keep ferns, or any humidity-loving plant. Without one, use the clues. Static shocks when you touch a door handle? Dry air. Cracked lips, dry skin, a nose that feels dry in the morning? Dry air. A fern with brown, crispy frond tips, in a room that feels dry to you, is telling you the same thing.

A pebble tray under the pot helps in the same way. Fill a shallow tray with pebbles, add water to just below the top of the pebbles, and sit the pot on top — the pot above the water, never in it. As the water evaporates, the air around the plant gets a little damper. A humidifier is the most reliable option, and in a dry, heated home it is the honest one. Misting helps a fern a little more than it helps most plants — the fine fronds catch the droplets — but it is still a short-lived effect, and it should never replace proper watering. A bright bathroom is often the best room in the house for a fern, as long as it gets enough light.

## Water: the soil half of humidity

Ferns want their soil moist all the time. Not wet, not soggy — moist, like a wrung-out sponge.

Check the soil every two or three days by touching the surface and pushing a finger in a couple of centimetres. If the surface feels dry, water. In a warm room in summer that may mean watering two or three times a week; in winter, once a week may be enough. Always check the soil rather than following a fixed schedule, because a fern in a hanging basket dries out faster than one in a pot on a shelf — air circulates all around it — and a fern in a cool room dries out slower than one in a warm one.

Water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer. Ferns are thirsty but they still rot if they sit in standing water. That sounds contradictory. It is not. The roots want damp soil with air in it, not soil that is waterlogged. A pot with drainage holes, and a saucer you empty every time, gives them that.

## Light and temperature through a humidity lens

Ferns evolved under a tree canopy, so they want bright light without direct sun. A north-facing window is often perfect. An east-facing window, with gentle morning sun, works well too. A spot a metre back from a south- or west-facing window is fine, but direct midday sun will scorch the fronds, turning them pale and then brown.

Too little light makes a fern grow slowly and produce thin, sparse fronds. If the plant looks pale and leggy, move it closer to a window. But light is rarely the main problem with ferns — moisture almost always is, and a fern in perfect light with dry air will still crisp.

Cold draughts are a problem too, but a different one. A sudden change — a cold draught, a move to a much drier room, or the soil drying out completely — can make a fern drop fronds. Ferns dislike sudden changes. Restore steady conditions and be patient. New fronds take weeks, not days.

## Soil, feeding, and the crown you must not cut

Ferns like a rich, moisture-retentive mix that still drains. A good houseplant compost with added perlite, or a mix with some coir or leaf mould, works well. The mix should hold water without becoming a soggy lump — if water sits on the surface for a long time after watering, the mix is too dense, and the roots will struggle for air.

Repot in spring when the roots fill the pot, going up one size. Ferns often produce a dense root ball; tease the edges gently rather than pulling it apart. After repotting, water well and keep the plant out of direct sun for a week. Some ferns, like the Boston fern, produce long runners — these can be trimmed off or used to start new plants. Ferns like to be slightly snug, so only go up one pot size. A pot far too large holds wet soil the roots cannot use, and the roots rot.

Feeding: less than you think. Ferns are light feeders. A weak liquid feed — half the strength on the label — once every four to six weeks in spring and summer is enough. Overfeeding burns the delicate frond tips and can leave brown marks. Never feed a dry fern; water first, then feed. In autumn and winter, stop feeding entirely.

Pruning is simple, and it has one rule. Remove dead or brown fronds by cutting them off at the base, close to the soil. Pulling them can damage the crown, which is where all new fronds emerge. Never cut the centre of the plant — the new fronds unfurl from there, and damaging them sets the plant back badly. Do not let the crown dry out, either. It is the most important part of the plant, and the most easily damaged.

## Where it usually goes wrong

Brown, crispy frond tips: dry air or underwatering. Check the soil first, then improve humidity — a pebble tray, grouping, or a humidifier. Crispy fronds will not turn green again. Cut them off and focus on the new growth.

Whole fronds turning brown and dry: the plant has dried out badly, or it sits in direct sun or above a radiator. Cut off the dead fronds, soak the pot, and move the plant.

Fronds going pale or yellow: often too much sun, or overwatering. Move it to shadier light and check the soil is not waterlogged.

No new growth in spring: usually too little light, or the plant is rootbound. Move it brighter, and check whether it needs repotting.

Dropping fronds: a sudden change — a cold draught, a move to a much drier room, or the soil drying out completely. Restore steady conditions and be patient.

Small insects on the fronds: ferns can get scale and mealybugs. Isolate the plant, wipe what you can reach, and remove badly affected fronds.

## Questions people ask

### How often should I water ferns indoors?

Every few days, whenever the soil surface starts to feel dry — ferns should never dry out completely. In a warm room in summer that may be two or three times a week; in winter, once a week. Always check the soil rather than following a fixed schedule, and empty the saucer after watering.

### Why are my fern's leaves turning brown and crispy?

Almost always dry air, underwatering, or a hot draught from a radiator. Check the soil moisture first, then raise the humidity around the plant with a pebble tray, grouping, or a humidifier. Crispy fronds will not turn green again — cut them off and focus on the new growth.

### Can ferns grow in low light?

Ferns tolerate lower light better than many houseplants, but they still need bright, indirect light to grow well. A north-facing window is ideal. In a very dark corner a fern will survive for a while but grow slowly and sparsely.

### Should I mist my fern?

Misting helps ferns a little — the fine fronds catch droplets — but it does not replace watering or raise humidity for long. It is a useful extra in a dry room, not a substitute for a pebble tray or humidifier. Never mist a fern that is sitting in still, cold air.

### How do I revive a dried-out fern?

Cut off the dead fronds, soak the pot in a basin of water for twenty minutes so the root ball rehydrates, then drain it fully. Return to regular watering and keep the humidity up. New fronds usually emerge from the centre within a few weeks if the crown is still alive.

### Do ferns need to be repotted often?

Every one to two years in spring, when the roots fill the pot. Ferns like to be slightly snug, so only go up one pot size. After repotting, water well and keep the plant out of direct sun while it settles.
