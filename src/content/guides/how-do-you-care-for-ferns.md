You care for ferns indoors by keeping the soil consistently moist, giving them bright indirect light, and keeping the air around them humid. Ferns are different from most houseplants: they have no flowers or seeds, they reproduce by spores, and they come from forest floors where the soil never fully dries out and the air is always damp. A fern that is allowed to dry out will crisp at the edges, drop fronds, and take a long time to recover.

The good news is that ferns are not fussy about much else. They do not need much feeding, they do not need direct sun, and they are happy in a cool room. The whole job is really moisture — in the soil and in the air. Get that right and a fern rewards you with a fountain of green fronds.

## At a glance

| Need | What ferns want |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light; a north or east window is ideal |
| Water | Keep soil evenly moist at all times — never let it dry out fully |
| Soil | Rich, loose, moisture-retentive mix with good drainage |
| Pot | A pot with drainage holes; hanging baskets and pedestals suit many ferns |
| Temperature | 15–24 °C; keep away from radiators and hot draughts |
| Humidity | 50% or higher is ideal; below 40% the fronds crisp |
| Feeding | Light feed every 4–6 weeks in spring and summer only |
| Difficulty | Moderate — easy once the watering habit is set, unforgiving of drought |

## Light: bright shade, not sun

Ferns evolved under a tree canopy, so they want bright light without direct sun. A north-facing window is often perfect. An east-facing window, with gentle morning sun, works well too. A spot a metre back from a south- or west-facing window is fine, but direct midday sun will scorch the fronds, turning them pale and then brown.

Too little light makes a fern grow slowly and produce thin, sparse fronds. If the plant looks pale and leggy, move it closer to a window. But light is rarely the main problem with ferns — moisture almost always is.

## Watering: the one rule that matters most

Ferns want their soil moist all the time. Not wet, not soggy — moist, like a wrung-out sponge. Check the soil every two or three days by touching the surface and pushing a finger in a couple of centimetres. If the surface feels dry, water. In a warm room in summer that may mean watering two or three times a week; in winter, once a week may be enough.

Water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer. Ferns are thirsty but they still rot if they sit in standing water. A fern in a hanging basket dries out faster than one in a pot on a shelf, because air circulates all around it — check hanging ferns more often.

If a fern does dry out badly, the fronds may crisp and die back. Do not give up immediately: cut off the dead fronds, soak the pot in a basin of water for twenty minutes, then return to regular watering. Ferns often regrow from the centre, although it can take weeks before new fronds unfurl.

## Humidity: the second half of the job

Most homes are too dry for ferns, especially in winter when the heating is on. Humidity below 40% will crisp the edges of the fronds. Ferns want 50% or more, and the usual household fixes work better here than for most plants because ferns genuinely respond to them.

Grouping plants together raises the humidity in the immediate area. A pebble tray under the pot helps. A humidifier is the most reliable option. Misting helps a fern a little more than it helps most plants — the fine fronds catch the droplets — but it is still a short-lived effect, and it should never replace proper watering. A bright bathroom is often the best room in the house for a fern, as long as it gets enough light.

## Soil, potting and repotting

Ferns like a rich, moisture-retentive mix that still drains. A good houseplant compost with added perlite, or a mix with some coir or leaf mould, works well. The mix should hold water without becoming a soggy lump — if water sits on the surface for a long time after watering, the mix is too dense.

Repot in spring when the roots fill the pot, going up one size. Ferns often produce a dense root ball; tease the edges gently rather than pulling it apart. After repotting, water well and keep the plant out of direct sun for a week. Some ferns, like the Boston fern, produce long runners — these can be trimmed off or used to start new plants.

## Feeding: less than you think

Ferns are light feeders. A weak liquid feed — half the strength on the label — once every four to six weeks in spring and summer is enough. Overfeeding burns the delicate frond tips and can leave brown marks. Never feed a dry fern; water first, then feed. In autumn and winter, stop feeding entirely.

## Pruning and cleaning

Remove dead or brown fronds by cutting them off at the base, close to the soil. Pulling them can damage the crown, which is where all new fronds emerge. Never cut the centre of the plant — the new fronds unfurl from there, and damaging them sets the plant back badly.

Ferns do not need leaf cleaning in the way broad-leaved plants do. A gentle shower in the sink or bath every month or two washes dust off the fronds and refreshes the plant. Let it drain fully before putting it back.

## Common mistakes

**Letting the soil dry out.** This is the number one fern killer. Ferns do not forgive drought the way a snake plant does. Check the soil every few days.

**Direct sun.** Scorched fronds do not recover. Bright, indirect light only.

**Hot, dry air from radiators.** A fern on a shelf above a radiator will crisp no matter how much you water it. Move it somewhere cooler.

**Overfeeding.** Brown frond tips are often fertiliser burn. Feed weakly and infrequently.

**Overpotting.** A pot far too large holds wet soil the roots cannot use, and the roots rot. Go up one size at a time.

**Ignoring the crown.** All new growth comes from the centre. Do not cut into it, and do not let it dry out.

## Troubleshooting

**Brown, crispy frond tips:** dry air or underwatering. Check the soil first, then improve humidity — a pebble tray, grouping, or a humidifier.

**Whole fronds turning brown and dry:** the plant has dried out badly, or it sits in direct sun or above a radiator. Cut off the dead fronds, soak the pot, and move the plant.

**Fronds going pale or yellow:** often too much sun, or overwatering. Move it to shadier light and check the soil is not waterlogged.

**No new growth in spring:** usually too little light, or the plant is rootbound. Move it brighter, and check whether it needs repotting.

**Dropping fronds:** a sudden change — a cold draught, a move to a much drier room, or the soil drying out completely. Ferns dislike sudden changes; restore steady conditions and be patient.

**Small insects on the fronds:** ferns can get scale and mealybugs. Isolate the plant, wipe what you can reach, and remove badly affected fronds.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I water ferns indoors?

Every few days, whenever the soil surface starts to feel dry — ferns should never dry out completely. In a warm room in summer that may be two or three times a week; in winter, once a week. Always check the soil rather than following a fixed schedule, and empty the saucer after watering.

### Why are my fern's leaves turning brown and crispy?

Almost always dry air, underwatering, or a hot draught from a radiator. Check the soil moisture first, then raise the humidity around the plant with a pebble tray, grouping, or a humidifier. Crispy fronds will not turn green again — cut them off and focus on the new growth.

### Can ferns grow in low light?

Ferns tolerate lower light better than many houseplants, but they still need bright, indirect light to grow well. A north-facing window is ideal. In a very dark corner a fern will survive for a while but grow slowly and sparsely.

### Should I mist my fern?

Misting helps ferns a little — the fine fronds catch droplets — but it does not replace watering or raise humidity for long. It is a useful extra in a dry room, not a substitute for a pebble tray or humidifier. Never mist a fern that is sitting in still, cold air.

### How do I revive a dried-out fern?

Cut off the dead fronds, soak the pot in a basin of water for twenty minutes so the root ball rehydrates, then drain it fully. Return to regular watering and keep the humidity up. New fronds usually emerge from the centre within a few weeks if the crown is still alive.

### Do ferns need to be repotted often?

Every one to two years in spring, when the roots fill the pot. Ferns like to be slightly snug, so only go up one pot size. After repotting, water well and keep the plant out of direct sun while it settles.

## Conclusion

Fern care is a moisture routine: water before the soil dries, keep the air humid, and keep the plant out of sun and radiator heat. Once that routine is in place, ferns are undemanding — a weak feed in summer, a shower now and then, and a trim of dead fronds. A fern that is never allowed to dry out will repay you with a full, green fountain of fronds for years.
