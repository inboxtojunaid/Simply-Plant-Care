Most poinsettias are thrown away on Boxing Day. Not because they died — because nobody told their owner what January looks like. The plant is still alive under those fading red bracts, and it can colour up again next Christmas, if you know what it needs in each month. The year breaks down cleanly, and the work is never heavy. It's just specific, and September asks more of you than any other month.

A few facts before the calendar starts. The red, pink or white "flowers" are actually coloured leaves, called bracts, with small yellow flowers in the centre. The plant comes from warm climates and dislikes cold: keep the room between 15°C and 22°C, keep it away from draughts, and never let it stand in water. Those three rules run all year. The rest changes by month.

## January: the comedown

January is a quiet month for a poinsettia, and it should be. Keep watering and giving it bright, indirect light — a spot near a sunny window, out of harsh midday sun. An east-facing window is ideal; a south- or west-facing window works if the plant sits a little back from the glass or behind a sheer curtain. In a dim corner the bracts fade faster and the lower leaves may drop, so give it the brightest spot you have.

Check the soil with your finger every few days. When the top 2–3 cm feel dry, water thoroughly until water runs from the drainage holes, then let the pot drain for 15–20 minutes and empty the saucer. Never let it stand in water. In a warm, bright room you may need to water once or twice a week; in a cool room, once a week or less is enough.

Leaf drop in January usually follows a shock: a cold draught, a cold journey home, overwatering, or underwatering. Check the soil moisture, then draughts, then temperature, and fix the most likely one. A plant that drops leaves from a cold shock may recover with steady warmth, but the dropped leaves won't grow back on bare stems. New growth comes from pruning, and that's February's job.

Use room-temperature water, and avoid splashing water onto the bracts, which can spot them.

## February–March: cut it back, keep it warm

When the bracts fade, cut the stems back to 10–15 cm in late winter. It looks brutal. It isn't. The plant responds by pushing out bushy new growth from the cut stems, and a pruned poinsettia ends up fuller and more attractive than one left to grow leggy.

Keep it warm — still 15–22°C, still away from the front door, an open window in winter, and an unheated hallway. A sudden cold draught can cause leaves to drop even if the plant was healthy the day before. Keep watering when the top soil dries, and keep it in bright light.

If the lower leaves are turning yellow now, that's usually overwatering or poor drainage. Let the soil dry out, make sure the pot drains, and remove the foil wrapping if it's still on.

## April–May: repot and wake it up

Spring is when the poinsettia wakes up properly. If it's root-bound, repot it now, into fresh free-draining houseplant mix in a pot one size larger. Start feeding lightly, every two to four weeks at half strength, and only in spring and summer.

Pinch the growing tips to encourage branching. Each pinch means two new shoots where one was growing, and a bushier plant by summer. Keep it in bright light. If you move it outdoors for the summer, do so gradually, starting in shade and moving into brighter light over a week or two — a plant moved straight from a living room to full sun will scorch.

And a note on the foil, since it still kills poinsettias in April as surely as it did in December: water collects inside it and the roots sit in a puddle. Either remove the foil entirely and use a saucer, or lift the plant out to water and drain it. If you haven't dealt with the foil yet, do it now.

## June–August: the summer stretch

Summer is the easy season. Let it grow in bright light, water when the top soil dries, keep pinching tips for a bushy shape, and keep feeding lightly every two to four weeks. If it's outdoors, give it sheltered bright indirect light, not baking sun all day, and bring it in before nights drop below 12°C.

Signs of overwatering are yellowing lower leaves and leaf drop; signs of underwatering are wilting and dry, crisp leaf edges. If the plant wilts from dryness, water it promptly — it usually recovers within hours. If it wilts while the soil is wet, the roots may be damaged: let the soil dry out and check the drainage rather than watering again.

By late August, stop pinching. The plant needs time to set its growth before the autumn routine begins, and new tips pinched off in September won't have time to mature before the dark nights start.

## September: the dark nights begin

This is the month that decides whether you get red bracts at Christmas. Poinsettias colour up when the nights get long, and indoors, you have to make the nights long yourself.

From late September, give the plant about 14 hours of uninterrupted darkness every night for around eight weeks, with bright light during the day. In practice, that means putting it in a dark cupboard or covering it with a light-proof box from about 5pm to 7am, every night, without fail. Even a brief exposure to light during the dark period — a lamp switched on in the room, a streetlight through the window — can delay or prevent colouring. Be strict.

This is the one stage where a poinsettia asks for real commitment. It's a nightly routine for two months, and missing nights stretches the timeline or stops it altogether. If that sounds like too much, there's no shame in enjoying the plant as a green houseplant and buying a new one in December. Many people do exactly that.

## October–November: colour, slowly

Keep the dark routine going through October. The bracts should begin to colour in six to eight weeks from the start of the long nights, so by late October or November you should see the first flush of red — or pink, or white, depending on the variety — at the tips.

Keep watering as before, keep it warm, keep it in bright light during the day. Don't let a lamp shine on it at night now that the colour is forming — the dark period still matters until the colour is well set.

If nothing is colouring by mid-November, the usual cause is light leaking into the dark period. Check the cupboard or box for gaps, check whether anyone's been switching a light on in the room, and check for streetlight through a window. Even a small leak, repeated nightly, is enough to hold the plant green.

## December: the payoff — and the foil trap

December is the payoff month, and also the month when new poinsettias are bought — so if you're buying one this year, choose well. Pick a plant with dense, dark green leaves right down to the base, and bright, unblemished coloured bracts. Avoid plants with yellowing lower leaves, drooping bracts, or a lot of fallen leaves on the bench, all of which suggest it has already been stressed.

Once home, take off the foil wrapping or punch drainage holes in the bottom. Foil is the most common killer of indoor poinsettias, because water collects inside it and the roots sit in a puddle. Either remove the foil entirely and use a saucer, or keep the foil as a cover and lift the plant out to water and drain it.

Keep it in bright indirect light, 15–22°C, water when the top 2–3 cm of soil dries, drain fully, and keep it away from cold draughts and radiators. A radiator's hot, dry air shortens the bracts' life and dries the soil too fast. A front door's cold draught can drop leaves overnight.

Then January comes around again. And this time, you know what it looks like.

## If it looks unhappy

Leaf drop usually follows a shock: cold draught, a cold journey home, overwatering, or underwatering. Work through the causes in order — soil moisture first, then draughts, then temperature. Yellow lower leaves usually mean overwatering or poor drainage: let the soil dry out, make sure the pot drains, remove the foil if it's still on. A wilting plant with wet soil suggests root damage from overwatering — stop watering, let the soil dry, and check that the pot drains freely. If the roots are brown and mushy, trim the damaged parts and repot in fresh, dry mix.

## Questions people ask

### How long do poinsettias last indoors?

With good care — bright light, careful watering and a warm, draught-free spot — a poinsettia's colour lasts two to three months, and the plant itself can live for years as a green houseplant if you keep it after Christmas.

### How often should I water a poinsettia inside?

When the top 2–3 cm of soil feels dry, usually once a week in a cool room and once or twice a week in a warm, bright room. Water thoroughly, let it drain, and empty the saucer. Never let it stand in water.

### Why is my poinsettia dropping leaves?

The most common causes are cold draughts, overwatering, and underwatering, in that order. Check the soil first, then check for draughts from doors and windows, and make sure the room stays above 15°C.

### How do I get my poinsettia to turn red again?

From late September, give it 14 hours of complete darkness every night for about eight weeks, with bright light during the day. A dark cupboard from 5pm to 7am works. Even brief light at night can stop the bracts colouring, so be strict.

### Can I keep a poinsettia as a green houseplant if I skip the dark nights?

Yes. Without the long dark nights the bracts stay green, and the plant grows as a leafy houseplant. That's a normal outcome, not a failure — the dark routine is only needed if you want the colour back.
