A poinsettia is not a disposable Christmas decoration — it is a living plant that can be kept indoors for years and brought back into red colour each winter, if you know what it needs. The direct answer is: give it bright, indirect light, keep it warm and away from draughts, water when the top soil dries, and let it drain fully. Most poinsettias die indoors because they are overwatered in a foil-wrapped pot with no drainage, or left in a cold draught by the front door.

This guide covers indoor care through the holidays and beyond: watering and light, temperature, what to do after Christmas, and the full year-long timeline if you want it to colour up again next winter.

## At a glance

| Factor | What a poinsettia needs indoors |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light — near a sunny window, out of harsh midday sun |
| Water | When the top 2–3 cm of soil dries; water thoroughly, then drain fully |
| Temperature | 15–22°C; keep away from cold draughts and radiators |
| Humidity | Normal household humidity is fine; avoid very dry, hot air |
| Soil | Free-draining houseplant mix |
| Pot | Must drain — remove foil wrapping or punch holes in it |
| Feeding | Lightly, in spring and summer only |
| Difficulty | Easy through Christmas; moderate if you want it to rebloom |

## Buying and bringing it home

Choose a plant with dense, dark green leaves right down to the base, and bright, unblemished coloured bracts — the red, pink or white "flowers" are actually coloured leaves, with the small yellow flowers in the centre. Avoid plants with yellowing lower leaves, drooping bracts, or a lot of fallen leaves on the bench, all of which suggest it has already been stressed.

Once home, take off the foil wrapping or punch drainage holes in the bottom — foil is the most common killer of indoor poinsettias, because water collects inside it and the roots sit in a puddle. Either remove the foil entirely and use a saucer, or keep the foil as a cover and lift the plant out to water and drain it.

## Light indoors

Poinsettias need bright, indirect light indoors — a spot near a sunny window, but not in harsh midday sun for hours. An east-facing window is ideal; a south- or west-facing window works if the plant sits a little back from the glass or behind a sheer curtain. In a dim corner the bracts will fade faster and the lower leaves may drop, so give it the brightest spot you have through the winter.

From spring onwards, the plant still wants bright light to grow well. If you move it outdoors for the summer, do so gradually, starting in shade and moving into brighter light over a week or two.

## Watering a poinsettia indoors

Watering is where most poinsettias are lost. The plant likes soil that is moist but never soggy, and it is less forgiving of overwatering than many houseplants.

Check the soil with your finger every few days. When the top 2–3 cm feel dry, water thoroughly until water runs from the drainage holes, then let the pot drain for 15–20 minutes and empty the saucer or foil. Do not let the plant stand in water. In a warm, bright room you may need to water once or twice a week; in a cool room, once a week or less is enough.

Signs of overwatering are yellowing lower leaves and leaf drop; signs of underwatering are wilting and dry, crisp leaf edges. If the plant wilts from dryness, water it promptly — it usually recovers within hours. If it wilts while the soil is wet, the roots may be damaged, and the best response is to let the soil dry out and check the drainage rather than watering again.

Use room-temperature water, and avoid splashing water onto the bracts, which can spot them.

## Temperature and draughts

Poinsettias come from warm climates and dislike cold. Keep the room between 15°C and 22°C, and keep the plant away from cold draughts — the front door, an open window in winter, and an unheated hallway are all risky spots. A sudden cold draught can cause leaves to drop even if the plant was healthy the day before.

## Getting it to colour up again

Reblooming a poinsettia is the part most people find mysterious, but the rule is simple: poinsettias colour up when the nights get long. From late September, the plant needs about 14 hours of uninterrupted darkness every night for around eight weeks, with bright light during the day.

In practice, that means putting the plant in a dark cupboard or covering it with a light-proof box from about 5pm to 7am, every night, without fail. Even a brief exposure to light during the dark period — a lamp switched on in the room, a streetlight through the window — can delay or prevent colouring. Be strict for eight weeks, and the bracts should begin to colour in six to eight weeks, ready for the holidays.

This is the one stage where a poinsettia asks for real commitment. If the dark-period routine sounds like too much, there is no shame in enjoying the plant as a green houseplant and buying a new one in December — many people do exactly that.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving the foil wrapping on with no drainage.** Water collects in the foil and the roots rot. Remove the foil or punch holes in it.
- **Leaving the plant in standing water.** Always empty the saucer after watering.
- **Putting it by the front door.** Cold draughts cause sudden leaf drop.
- **Placing it next to a radiator.** Hot, dry air shortens the bracts' life and dries the soil too fast.
- **Overwatering to "help" a wilting plant.** If the soil is already wet, more water makes root damage worse. Check the soil first.
- **Expecting it to recolour without long nights.** Without 14 hours of darkness each night in autumn, the bracts will stay green. That is not a failure of the plant — it is how it works.

## Troubleshooting

### The leaves are dropping

Leaf drop usually follows a shock: cold draught, a cold journey home, overwatering, or underwatering. Work through the causes in order — check the soil moisture, check for draughts, check the temperature — and fix the most likely one. A poinsettia that drops leaves from a cold shock may recover with steady warmth and careful watering, but the dropped leaves will not grow back on bare stems; new growth comes from pruning in spring.

### The lower leaves are turning yellow

Yellow lower leaves usually mean overwatering or poor drainage. Let the soil dry out, make sure the pot drains, and remove the foil if it is still on. A few yellow leaves at the base of an older plant are normal, but widespread yellowing is a watering problem.

### The plant is wilting but the soil is wet

This suggests root damage from overwatering. Stop watering, let the soil dry out, and check that the pot drains freely. If the roots are brown and mushy, trim the damaged parts and repot in fresh, dry mix.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long do poinsettias last indoors?

With good care — bright light, careful watering and a warm, draught-free spot — a poinsettia's colour lasts two to three months, and the plant itself can live for years as a green houseplant if you keep it after Christmas.

### How often should I water a poinsettia inside?

When the top 2–3 cm of soil feels dry, usually once a week in a cool room and once or twice a week in a warm, bright room. Water thoroughly, let it drain, and empty the saucer. Never let it stand in water.

### Why is my poinsettia dropping leaves?

The most common causes are cold draughts, overwatering, and underwatering, in that order. Check the soil first, then check for draughts from doors and windows, and make sure the room stays above 15°C.

### How do I get my poinsettia to turn red again?

From late September, give it 14 hours of complete darkness every night for about eight weeks, with bright light during the day. A dark cupboard from 5pm to 7am works. Even brief light at night can stop the bracts colouring, so be strict.

## Conclusion

A poinsettia kept indoors is not difficult — it is just particular about three things: drainage, warmth, and light. Remove the foil, water only when the top soil dries and let it drain, keep it warm and out of draughts, and give it bright, indirect light. Do that and it will carry its colour through the holidays. Keep it after Christmas, prune it in spring, and give it long dark nights in autumn, and it will colour up again next year — a plant you bought once, enjoyed twice.
