You take care of plants by giving them the right amount of light, watering only when the soil needs it, using a pot with drainage, and checking on them regularly. That is the whole routine in one sentence — and it is the routine that keeps most houseplants alive. The details matter, but they are not complicated. Once you learn to read your plant and your soil, the guesswork mostly disappears.

Most beginner plant deaths come from the same two mistakes: too much water, and too little light. Everything in this guide is built around avoiding those two, plus a few small habits that keep a plant healthy over months rather than weeks.

## At a glance

| Need | What most houseplants want |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light near a window; a few hours of gentle morning sun suits many |
| Water | Water thoroughly, then let the top 2–5 cm of soil dry before watering again |
| Soil | Loose, free-draining potting mix, never heavy garden soil |
| Pot | A pot with drainage holes and a saucer; empty the saucer after watering |
| Temperature | 15–25 °C, away from radiators, cold draughts and air-conditioning blasts |
| Feeding | Diluted liquid feed every 2–4 weeks in spring and summer only |
| Difficulty | Easy — a weekly check is enough for most plants |

## The beginner routine

### 1. Put it in the right light

Light is food for a plant. Before you do anything else, find the brightest spot in your home that does not get harsh midday sun straight through the glass. A spot within a metre or two of an east- or west-facing window suits most houseplants. A south-facing window is usually fine too if the plant sits slightly back from the glass or behind a sheer curtain.

Watch the plant for a week or two. Stems stretching towards the window, small pale leaves and slow growth usually mean it needs more light. Scorched brown patches or faded, washed-out leaves usually mean too much direct sun. Move the plant gradually — it shows the effect of a new spot over weeks, not hours.

### 2. Water by checking the soil, not by the calendar

There is no universal "water every Tuesday" rule, because a plant in a sunny spot in summer dries out far faster than the same plant in a dim corner in winter. The reliable method is the finger test: push a finger about 2–5 cm into the soil. If it feels dry at that depth, water. If it still feels damp, wait and check again in a few days.

When you do water, water thoroughly. Pour slowly and evenly until water runs out of the drainage holes, then let the pot drain and tip away any water sitting in the saucer. A plant left standing in water will develop root rot — roots that sit wet for too long begin to decay, and a rotted root system cannot take up water at all, which is why an overwatered plant often looks wilted even though the soil is wet.

### 3. Use the right pot and soil

Almost every houseplant sold in a shop comes in a plastic nursery pot inside a decorative outer pot. That is fine — but always lift the inner pot out after watering and empty the outer one. A plant with no drainage hole at all is a plant waiting to be overwatered.

Houseplants want a loose potting mix that holds some moisture but lets excess water drain away. Standard houseplant compost works for most. Heavy garden soil is too dense for pots: it compacts, holds too much water and starves the roots of air. If you repot, choose a pot only a little larger than the current one — a much bigger pot holds a lot of wet soil the roots cannot use yet.

### 4. Feed lightly, only in the growing season

Plants in pots cannot find nutrients the way garden plants can, so they need a little help — but only while they are actively growing, in spring and summer. A balanced liquid houseplant feed, diluted to half the strength on the label, every two to four weeks is plenty for most plants. More is not better: overfeeding can burn roots and leave a crust of salts on the soil surface.

### 5. Keep the leaves clean

Dust on leaves blocks light, and a plant with dusty leaves photosynthesises less well. Every few weeks, wipe broad leaves with a damp cloth. For small-leaved or hairy plants, a soft brush or a gentle shower in the sink works better. While you are at it, look at the undersides of the leaves — that is where pests usually appear first.

## A weekly check that takes five minutes

You do not need to fuss over your plants every day. A short weekly check is enough:

- Touch the soil of each plant. Water only the ones that are dry at finger depth.
- Turn each pot a quarter turn so all sides get light and the plant grows evenly.
- Look for yellowing leaves, drooping, or spots, and remove dead leaves.
- Check the saucers and empty any standing water.

That is it. Plants mostly want consistency, not constant attention. Many beginners kill plants by over-caring — watering on a schedule, moving the plant around, feeding too often — rather than by neglect.

## Common mistakes

**Watering on a fixed schedule.** A plant's water needs change with the season, the room, and the pot. Always check the soil first.

**No drainage.** A pot without holes, or a decorative outer pot that is never emptied, is the fastest route to root rot.

**Too little light.** A plant in a dark corner will slowly starve.

**Repotting into a huge pot.** A pot that is far too big stays wet for too long. Go up one size at a time.

**Moving the plant constantly.** Plants adjust slowly. Give a new spot at least two or three weeks before deciding it is wrong.

**Feeding a struggling plant.** Fertiliser does not rescue a sick plant — it adds stress. Fix light and water first.

## Troubleshooting

**Yellow leaves, soft stems, wet soil:** almost certainly overwatering. Let the soil dry out fully, remove mushy leaves, and check that water can drain. If the roots smell sour and are dark and mushy, the plant has root rot and needs the dead roots trimmed and fresh dry mix.

**Brown, crispy leaf edges:** usually dry air, underwatering, or salts building up in the soil. Water thoroughly so excess salts flush out of the drainage holes, and consider whether the plant sits near a radiator.

**Drooping leaves:** check the soil. Dry soil means the plant is thirsty — water and it should perk up within hours. Wet soil with drooping leaves is a warning sign of root trouble, not thirst.

**Leggy growth with small, pale leaves:** not enough light. Move the plant closer to a window.

**Tiny insects or sticky leaves:** isolate the plant, wipe the leaves, and inspect the undersides. Most common houseplant pests can be controlled by washing the plant and removing badly affected leaves, but keep the plant away from your others until it is clear.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I water my indoor plants?

As often as the soil needs it — which depends on the plant, the pot, the light and the season. Check the soil with your finger every few days and water only when the top few centimetres feel dry. For most houseplants in average conditions, that works out to roughly once a week in summer and once every two weeks in winter, but the soil check matters more than any schedule.

### How do I know if my plant needs more light?

The signs are slow: stems stretching towards the window, new leaves smaller and paler than the old ones, and little or no growth in spring and summer. Move the plant closer to a window and give it a few weeks — new growth should come in stronger and more compact.

### Why are the leaves on my plant turning yellow?

The most common cause is overwatering, especially if the soil stays wet and the lower leaves yellow first. It can also be natural ageing — the oldest leaf on a plant yellowing and dropping now and then is normal. If several leaves yellow at once, check your watering and drainage before anything else.

### Should I mist my houseplants?

For most ordinary houseplants, misting is not necessary and does little for humidity — the moisture evaporates in minutes. A few humidity-loving plants benefit from a pebble tray or being grouped together. Wiping leaves with a damp cloth does more practical good than misting.

### Do I need to repot a new plant straight away?

Usually not. A shop-bought plant is generally fine in its nursery pot for months. Repot when you see roots circling the bottom, poking out of the drainage holes, or when water runs straight through because the pot is mostly roots. Spring is the best time to repot.

### What is the easiest plant for a complete beginner?

A snake plant is the classic first choice: it tolerates low light, forgiving watering and neglect, and grows slowly. Pothos and ZZ plants are also good starters. Choose a plant that suits the light you actually have, not the light you wish you had.

## Conclusion

Taking care of plants is mostly about paying attention rather than doing a lot. Give your plant decent light, water when the soil is dry rather than on a schedule, keep it in a pot that drains, and feed it lightly while it is growing. Start with one or two forgiving plants, get the weekly check into a habit, and add more once the routine feels easy.
