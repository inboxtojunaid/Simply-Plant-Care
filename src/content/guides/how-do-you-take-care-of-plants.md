Two mistakes kill most beginner houseplants. Too much water. Too little light.

That is it. Not pests, not feeding, not some mysterious green thumb you either have or you do not. Water and light, in that order, and everything else is a footnote for the first few months. Once you get those two right, a plant will forgive a lot of small errors. Get them wrong, and no amount of fertiliser or misting will save it.

So this guide is not a list of rules to memorise. It is a week in the life of a new plant owner — what to do on day one, what to check midweek, what to do at the weekend, and what changes once the routine settles in. Follow the week a couple of times and it stops feeling like a routine at all.

## Day one: find the light first, water second

Before you do anything else, find the brightest spot in your home that does not get harsh midday sun straight through the glass.

A spot within a metre or two of an east- or west-facing window suits most houseplants. A south-facing window is usually fine too if the plant sits slightly back from the glass, or behind a sheer curtain. What you are looking for is bright, indirect light — the plant can see the sky, but the sun is not blazing directly onto its leaves for hours.

Put the plant in its spot. Leave it there. Plants adjust slowly, over weeks, not hours, so resist the urge to move it again tomorrow.

Then check the soil. Push a finger about 2–5 cm into the mix. Damp? Leave it alone. Dry at that depth? Water it properly: pour slowly and evenly until water runs out of the drainage holes, let the pot drain, and tip away any water sitting in the saucer. That last step matters more than people think. A plant left standing in water will develop root rot — roots that sit wet for too long begin to decay, and a rotted root system cannot take up water at all, which is why an overwatered plant often looks wilted even though the soil is wet.

That is day one. Spot, soil check, water if needed, empty the saucer. Done.

## Tuesday or Wednesday: the midweek look

You do not need to do anything to your plant midweek. Just look at it.

Seriously. Walk past, glance at the leaves, notice whether anything has changed. This is the habit that separates people whose plants live from people whose plants die quietly — not fussing, just noticing.

What are you looking for? Stems stretching towards the window, small pale leaves, and slow growth usually mean it needs more light. Scorched brown patches, or faded, washed-out leaves, usually mean too much direct sun. Drooping leaves with dry soil mean thirst. Drooping leaves with wet soil mean root trouble, not thirst — do not water that plant again.

## The weekend: the five-minute check

Saturday or Sunday, whenever you have five minutes, do the weekly check. This is the whole routine, and it is short.

Touch the soil of each plant. Water only the ones that are dry at finger depth. Water thoroughly, until it runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer.

Turn each pot a quarter turn, so all sides get light and the plant grows evenly instead of leaning towards the window.

Look for yellowing leaves, drooping, or spots, and remove dead leaves. A dead leaf left on the plant does nothing but rot and attract trouble.

Check the saucers and empty any standing water.

That is it. Four things, five minutes, once a week.

Plants mostly want consistency, not constant attention. Many beginners kill plants by over-caring — watering on a schedule, moving the plant around, feeding too often — rather than by neglect. A plant that gets a calm, regular check once a week will outlive a plant that gets fussed over daily.

## Week two: the same check, with a few additions

Week two looks a lot like week one. That is the point. But now you know what "dry at finger depth" feels like in your home, in your pots, in this season — and that knowledge is worth more than any watering schedule.

There is no universal "water every Tuesday" rule, because a plant in a sunny spot in summer dries out far faster than the same plant in a dim corner in winter. The finger test is the schedule. Push a finger 2–5 cm in. Dry? Water. Damp? Wait and check again in a few days.

This week, add two small habits.

First, clean the leaves. Dust on leaves blocks light, and a plant with dusty leaves photosynthesises less well. Every few weeks, wipe broad leaves with a damp cloth. For small-leaved or hairy plants, a soft brush or a gentle shower in the sink works better. While you are at it, you are already looking at the leaf undersides for pests — two jobs, one pass.

And never feed a struggling plant. Fertiliser does not rescue a sick plant — it adds stress. Fix light and water first, wait for new growth, and only then think about feeding.

## Weeks three and four: you start to read the plant

By the third or fourth week, something shifts. You stop following instructions and start reading the plant itself.

You know how heavy the pot feels when the soil is dry versus wet. You know which of your plants dries out first — the one in the sunny spot, the one in the small terracotta pot — and which one stays damp for ages. You notice new growth, or the lack of it, and you know what that means.

Slow growth in spring and summer, with small pale leaves and stems stretching towards the window, means more light. Move the plant closer to a window and give it a few weeks. New growth should come in stronger and more compact.

Yellow leaves, soft stems, wet soil: almost certainly overwatering. Let the soil dry out fully, remove mushy leaves, and check that water can drain. If the roots smell sour and are dark and mushy, the plant has root rot — trim the dead roots and repot into fresh, dry mix.

Leggy growth with small, pale leaves: not enough light. Move the plant closer to a window.

Tiny insects or sticky leaves: isolate the plant, wipe the leaves, and inspect the undersides. Most common houseplant pests can be controlled by washing the plant and removing badly affected leaves, but keep the plant away from your others until it is clear.

## What changes with the seasons

The routine stays the same all year. The timing changes.

In autumn, growth slows. Stop feeding. Water less often. The same plant that needed water every week in July may need it every two weeks in October, and that is normal.

In winter, most houseplants barely grow at all. Water much less — check the soil, and expect it to stay damp for longer. Keep plants away from radiators, which dry the air and the soil, and away from cold draughts and air-conditioning blasts. A plant that sits in a draught all winter will show it in spring, if it shows it at all.

## The mistakes that undo a good week

Watering on a fixed schedule. A plant's water needs change with the season, the room, and the pot. Always check the soil first.

No drainage. A pot without holes, or a decorative outer pot that is never emptied, is the fastest route to root rot.

Too little light. A plant in a dark corner will slowly starve. It will not complain. It will just fade.

Repotting into a huge pot. A pot that is far too big stays wet for too long. Go up one size at a time.

Moving the plant constantly. Plants adjust slowly. Give a new spot at least two or three weeks before deciding it is wrong.

Feeding a struggling plant. Fertiliser does not rescue a sick plant. Fix light and water first.

If you avoid those seven, and you do the five-minute weekend check, you are already doing better than most new plant owners. The rest is detail you can learn one plant at a time.

## Questions people ask

### How often should I water my indoor plants?

As often as the soil needs it — which depends on the plant, the pot, the light and the season. Check the soil with your finger every few days and water only when the top few centimetres feel dry. For most houseplants in average conditions, that works out to roughly once a week in summer and once every two weeks in winter, but the soil check matters more than any schedule.

### How do I know if my plant needs more light?

The signs are slow: stems stretching towards the window, new leaves smaller and paler than the old ones, and little or no growth in spring and summer. Move the plant closer to a window and give it a few weeks — new growth should come in stronger and more compact.

### Why are the leaves on my plant turning yellow?

The most common cause is overwatering, especially if the soil stays wet and the lower leaves yellow first. It can also be natural ageing — the oldest leaf on a plant yellowing and dropping now and then is normal. If several leaves yellow at once, check your watering and drainage before anything else.

### Should I mist my houseplants?

For most ordinary houseplants, misting is not necessary and does little for humidity — the moisture evaporates in minutes. A few humidity-loving plants benefit from a pebble tray or being grouped together. Wiping leaves with a damp cloth does more practical good than misting.

### Do I need to repot a new plant straight away?

Usually not. A shop-bought plant is generally fine in its nursery pot for months. Repot when you see roots circling the bottom, poking out of the drainage holes, or when water runs straight through because the pot is mostly roots. Spring is the best time to repot.
