Two to three months. That is how long a single anthurium flower can last — not days, like most houseplant blooms, but months.

The catch is that the plant will only do that trick when it is happy, and an unhappy anthurium does not hide it. It flags the problem on its leaves first: brown tips, yellow leaves, a flower that turns green instead of staying red. So this guide starts where most guides end — with the symptoms — and works backwards to the routine that prevents them. Find your symptom below, fix it, then read on for the care that keeps it from coming back.

## Brown tips? Start here

Brown, crispy leaf tips are the most common anthurium complaint, and they almost always mean one of two things: dry air, or salts building up in the soil.

Dry air is the usual suspect, especially in heated homes in winter. Anthuriums come from tropical forests, where the air rarely dries out, and they want humidity around 50–60%. Most heated homes in winter sit well below that — often under 30% — and the leaf tips are the first part of the plant to show it. They crisp, they brown, and they do not heal. You can trim the brown edge off with clean scissors, following the shape of the leaf, but the plant will not regrow that tip.

Salt build-up is the quieter cause. Hard tap water and fertiliser leave salts in the soil, and those salts burn the leaf tips in exactly the same way. If you have been feeding a plant that is barely growing, or watering with hard tap water for months without ever flushing the soil, salts are worth suspecting. The fix is simple: water thoroughly with plain water until it runs freely from the drainage holes, flushing the excess salts out, and ease off the feeding.

One thing that does not fix brown tips: misting. Misting raises the humidity around the leaves for a few minutes, then it evaporates. It does not meaningfully change the air the plant lives in, and wet leaves left in still air can invite fungal spots. It feels helpful. It changes almost nothing.

## Yellow leaves, no flowers, green flowers — what each one means

Yellow leaves, especially lower ones, usually mean overwatering. The mix is staying wet, the roots are struggling, and the plant is shedding its oldest leaves first. Let the mix dry more between waterings, check that water can drain freely, and empty the saucer every time. If the roots smell sour and are dark and mushy, the plant has root rot — trim the dead roots and repot into fresh, dry, chunky mix.

No flowers is a different problem, and it is almost always too little light. An anthurium in a dark corner will grow leaves happily for months and never produce a flower. It is not broken. It is just not getting enough energy to bloom. Move it closer to a window — bright, indirect light, no direct midday sun — and be patient. Flower production takes time, and a plant that has been in a dark corner may take a season to get going again.

Green flowers are the oddest symptom, and the most confusing. The colourful part of an anthurium "flower" is actually a modified leaf called a spathe, and the true flowers are the tiny bumps on the central spike, or spadix. When a spathe turns green instead of staying red or pink, the plant is usually getting too much light, or too much fertiliser. Move the plant slightly further from the window and ease off feeding. The green spathe will not turn red again — cut the flower stalk off at the base once it fades, and the next flower should come in with its proper colour.

Dark, soft patches on leaves mean something more urgent: cold damage, or rot. Anthuriums hate cold. Below 15 °C they sulk, and a cold draught can damage leaves in a single night. Move the plant somewhere warmer, remove damaged leaves, and check the roots if the soil is wet. Keep anthuriums away from cold draughts, open windows in winter, and radiators — the first chills, the second dries the air and the soil.

## Once it is stable: light that makes it flower

An anthurium wants bright light without direct sun. An east-facing window is ideal; a spot a metre or so back from a south- or west-facing window works too. Direct sun scorches the leaves, leaving bleached or brown patches that do not heal.

Too little light is the most common reason an anthurium stops flowering, as above. Too much light is the most common reason for scorched patches and green flowers. The sweet spot is wider than people think — bright, indirect light, where the plant can see the sky but the sun is not hitting its leaves directly for hours — but it is worth finding, because light is what decides whether you get flowers or just leaves.

## Watering and that chunky mix: moist, never soggy

Anthuriums like their soil lightly moist, but they rot quickly if it stays wet. Water when the top 3–5 cm of the mix feels dry, then water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes and empty the saucer. In winter, when growth slows, water less often and let the soil dry a little more between waterings.

Because anthuriums grow in a chunky, airy mix, they dry out faster than a plant in dense compost. Check the soil twice a week in summer rather than assuming a weekly water is enough. A plant in a cold, dim spot will use very little water — always check the soil rather than watering on a schedule.

The mix matters, and it is worth getting right. In the wild, anthuriums often grow on trees or in loose leaf litter, with air moving freely around their roots. Ordinary dense potting compost is a poor choice for them: it stays wet and airless, and the roots rot. Use a loose mix — roughly equal parts orchid bark, perlite and houseplant compost works well, or a ready-made aroid mix.

Repot every two to three years in spring, or sooner if the roots are filling the pot and water runs straight through. Go up one pot size only, and do not bury the stem deeper than it was. After repotting, water lightly and keep the plant out of direct sun for a week while it settles.

## Humidity without the misting myth

Humidity is the part of anthurium care that causes the most confusion, because the most commonly suggested fix — misting — is also the least effective, as noted above. What works instead is raising the humidity of the air itself, even slightly.

Grouping plants together raises the humidity in the immediate area. Placing the pot on a pebble tray with water helps — the pot sitting above the water, not in it, because a pot sitting in water will rot the roots. Running a small humidifier nearby helps most of all, especially in a dry, heated home. A bathroom with a window is often a good home for an anthurium, as long as it gets enough light. A dark bathroom is not suitable, no matter how humid it is — the plant still needs light to flower.

## Feeding for flowers that last

Anthuriums flower best when they are fed lightly and regularly in the growing season. Use a balanced liquid feed, or one slightly higher in phosphorus, diluted to half strength, every four to six weeks from spring to early autumn. Do not feed in winter, and never feed a dry plant — water first.

A single spathe can last two to three months. When one fades, cut the whole flower stalk off at the base — this encourages the plant to produce another. If you leave a faded flower on the plant, it will keep drawing energy that could go into the next one.

## Questions people ask

### How often should I water an anthurium?

When the top 3–5 cm of the mix feels dry — roughly once a week in summer, less in winter. Because anthuriums grow in a chunky mix that dries faster than dense compost, check the soil rather than following a fixed schedule, and always empty the saucer after watering.

### Why does my anthurium have brown leaf tips?

Dry air is the usual cause, especially in heated homes in winter. Salts from hard tap water or overfeeding can also crisp the tips. Improve humidity with a pebble tray or humidifier, and flush the soil with plain water now and then.

### How do I get my anthurium to flower again?

Give it bright, indirect light, feed lightly in spring and summer, and remove spent flower stalks at the base. A plant in too little light will grow leaves but no flowers. Flowering takes time — allow several weeks after moving the plant to a brighter spot.

### Can an anthurium live in a bathroom?

Yes, if the bathroom has a window with bright, indirect light. Bathrooms are often naturally humid, which anthuriums love. A dark bathroom is not suitable, no matter how humid it is — the plant still needs light to flower.

### Is misting good for anthurium?

Misting does little on its own. The moisture evaporates within minutes, so it does not raise the humidity the plant actually experiences, and wet leaves in still air can encourage fungal spots. A pebble tray, grouping plants together, or a humidifier are all more effective.

### When should I repot an anthurium?

Every two to three years in spring, or sooner if roots fill the pot and water runs straight through. Use a loose, bark-based mix and a pot one size larger. Avoid burying the stem deeper than it was before.
