You care for an anthurium by giving it bright, indirect light, watering when the top few centimetres of soil dry out, and keeping the air around it reasonably humid. Get those three right and an anthurium can flower for months at a time — its glossy red, pink or white spathes are among the longest-lasting blooms of any houseplant. Get the humidity wrong, and the first sign is usually brown, crispy leaf tips.

Anthuriums come from tropical forests, where they grow in the dappled light under trees and in air that rarely dries out. They are not difficult plants, but they do have clear preferences: they hate cold, they hate wet feet, and they hate dry air. A plant that is unhappy will usually tell you through its leaves long before it stops flowering.

## At a glance

| Need | What anthurium wants |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light; no direct midday sun |
| Water | Water when the top 3–5 cm of soil is dry; never leave standing in water |
| Soil | Very loose, chunky mix — orchid bark and perlite with some potting mix |
| Pot | A pot with drainage holes; repot every 2–3 years |
| Temperature | 18–28 °C; keep above 15 °C at all times |
| Humidity | 50–60% is ideal; 40% is workable with care |
| Feeding | Diluted feed every 4–6 weeks in spring and summer |
| Difficulty | Moderate — forgiving of light mistakes, less forgiving of cold and wet roots |

## Light: bright but never harsh

An anthurium wants bright light without direct sun. An east-facing window is ideal; a spot a metre or so back from a south- or west-facing window works too. Direct sun scorches the leaves, leaving bleached or brown patches that do not heal.

Too little light is the most common reason an anthurium stops flowering. If the plant is healthy and green but has not produced a flower in months, light is the first thing to check. Move it closer to a window and give it a few weeks — flower production takes time, and a plant that has been in a dark corner may take a season to get going again.

## Watering: moist, never soggy

Anthuriums like their soil lightly moist but they rot quickly if it stays wet. Water when the top 3–5 cm of the mix feels dry, then water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes and empty the saucer. In winter, when growth slows, water less often and let the soil dry a little more between waterings.

Because anthuriums grow in a chunky, airy mix (see below), they dry out faster than a plant in dense compost. Check the soil twice a week in summer rather than assuming a weekly water is enough. On the other hand, a plant in a cold, dim spot will use very little water — always check the soil rather than watering on a schedule.

## Soil and potting: airy and chunky

In the wild, anthuriums often grow on trees or in loose leaf litter, with air moving freely around their roots. That is why ordinary dense potting compost is a poor choice for them: it stays wet and airless, and the roots rot. Use a loose mix — roughly equal parts orchid bark, perlite and houseplant compost works well, or a ready-made aroid mix.

Repot every two to three years in spring, or sooner if the roots are filling the pot and water runs straight through. Go up one pot size only, and do not bury the stem deeper than it was. After repotting, water lightly and keep the plant out of direct sun for a week while it settles.

## Humidity: what actually works

Humidity is the part of anthurium care that causes the most confusion, because the most commonly suggested fix — misting — is also the least effective. Misting raises the humidity around the leaves for a few minutes, then it evaporates. It does not meaningfully change the air the plant lives in, and wet leaves left in still air can invite fungal spots.

What works instead is raising the humidity of the air itself, even slightly. Grouping plants together, placing the pot on a pebble tray with water (the pot sitting above the water, not in it), or running a small humidifier nearby all help. A bathroom with a window is often a good home for an anthurium, as long as it gets enough light.

## Feeding and flowering

Anthuriums flower best when they are fed lightly and regularly in the growing season. Use a balanced liquid feed, or one slightly higher in phosphorus, diluted to half strength, every four to six weeks from spring to early autumn. Do not feed in winter, and never feed a dry plant — water first.

The colourful part of an anthurium "flower" is actually a modified leaf called a spathe, and the true flowers are the tiny bumps on the central spike, or spadix. A single spathe can last two to three months. When one fades, cut the whole flower stalk off at the base — this encourages the plant to produce another. If a flower turns green instead of staying red or pink, the plant is usually getting too much light or too much fertiliser.

## Cleaning and general care

Wipe the broad leaves with a damp cloth every few weeks. Dust dulls the glossy leaves and blocks light, and a clean plant is much easier to inspect for pests. Remove yellow or dead leaves by cutting them off at the base of the stem, and remove spent flower stalks the same way.

Keep anthuriums away from cold draughts, open windows in winter, and radiators. A sudden chill can mark the leaves with dark patches. They also dislike being moved around often — once you find a spot with the right light, leave the plant there.

## Common mistakes

**Watering on a schedule without checking the soil.** Anthuriums rot fast in wet mix. Always check first.

**Dense, heavy soil.** Standard compost alone holds too much water. A chunky, bark-based mix is worth the effort.

**Direct sun.** Scorched patches do not heal. Bright, indirect light only.

**Misting as the only humidity fix.** Misting feels helpful but changes almost nothing. Use a pebble tray, grouping, or a humidifier instead.

**Cold rooms.** Below 15 °C an anthurium sulks, and a cold draught can damage leaves in a single night.

**Overfeeding.** Too much fertiliser burns leaf tips and can turn flowers green. Half strength, monthly, in the growing season only.

## Troubleshooting

**Brown, crispy leaf tips:** usually dry air, or salts building up from tap water and fertiliser. Improve humidity with a pebble tray or humidifier, and flush the soil with plain water occasionally.

**Yellow leaves, especially lower ones:** usually overwatering. Let the mix dry more between waterings and check the drainage.

**No flowers:** almost always too little light. Move the plant brighter and be patient — it can take weeks to start a new flower.

**Green flowers:** too much light or too much fertiliser. Move the plant slightly further from the window and ease off feeding.

**Dark, soft patches on leaves:** cold damage or rot. Move the plant somewhere warmer, remove damaged leaves, and check the roots if the soil is wet.

**Small insects on leaf undersides:** anthuriums can attract aphids and thrips. Isolate the plant, wipe the leaves, and remove badly affected foliage.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I water an anthurium?

When the top 3–5 cm of the mix feels dry — roughly once a week in summer, less in winter. Because anthuriums grow in a chunky mix that dries faster than dense compost, check the soil rather than following a fixed schedule, and always empty the saucer after watering.

### Why does my anthurium have brown leaf tips?

Dry air is the usual cause, especially in heated homes in winter. Salts from hard tap water or overfeeding can also crisp the tips. Improve humidity with a pebble tray or humidifier, and flush the soil with plain water now and then.

### How do I get my anthurium to flower again?

Give it bright, indirect light, feed lightly in spring and summer, and remove spent flower stalks at the base. A plant in too little light will grow leaves but no flowers. Flowering takes time — allow several weeks after moving the plant to a brighter spot.

### Can an anthurium live in a bathroom?

Yes, if the bathroom has a window with bright, indirect light. Bathrooms are often naturally humid, which anthuriums love. A dark bathroom is not suitable, no matter how humid it is — the plant still needs light to flower.

### Is misting good for anthurium?

Misting does little on its own. The moisture evaporates within minutes, so it does not raise the humidity the plant actually experiences, and wet leaves in still air can encourage fungal spots. A pebble tray, grouping plants together, or a humidifier are all more effective.

### When should I repot an anthurium?

Every two to three years in spring, or sooner if roots fill the pot and water runs straight through. Use a loose, bark-based mix and a pot one size larger. Avoid burying the stem deeper than it was before.

## Conclusion

Anthurium care is really about recreating a small piece of tropical forest: bright light without direct sun, a loose airy mix that drains freely, and air that is not too dry. Get the light right and the plant flowers; get the humidity right and the leaves stay glossy and unblemished. Once it is settled in a good spot, an anthurium asks for very little — a weekly soil check, a light feed in summer, and the occasional wipe of its leaves.
