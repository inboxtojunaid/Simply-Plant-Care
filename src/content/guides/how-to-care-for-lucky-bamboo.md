First, the name is a lie. Lucky bamboo is not bamboo at all. It is a dracaena, a tropical plant sold as bare stalks in a vase of water, sometimes trained into curls, braids, or tiered shapes. It needs no soil, very little light, and almost no attention — which is why it ends up on desks and shelves everywhere. It is also one of the most misunderstood houseplants, because the two things that matter most are the ones nobody tells you about: the water has to be right, and the light has to be gentle.

And there is a choice to make, right at the start. Most lucky bamboo is sold growing in water, and it can live that way happily for years. It can also be planted in soil, and many growers find soil-grown plants are sturdier and live longer. Neither is wrong. They just need different care, and the comparison below — and the rest of this guide — runs through those differences so you can pick the one that fits how you actually look after plants.

## Water or soil? The honest comparison

Start with water, because that is how most people meet the plant. A vase of water looks clean and simple, and it is — but the water is the whole growing medium, so its quality is everything. It needs changing every one to two weeks, it needs to be the right kind of water, and the roots must stay submerged. Forget for a month and the water goes stale, grows algae, starts to smell, and the roots suffer first. Water-grown plants are also more sensitive to what is in the tap: chlorine and fluoride yellow the leaves from the tips inwards, which is the classic sign of a water problem.

Soil is the steadier option. A pot of ordinary potting mix with drainage holds moisture more evenly, buffers the plant against a forgotten week, and lets the roots breathe in a way a vase never quite does. Soil-grown plants are often sturdier, can grow taller, and cope better if you go away for a fortnight. The trade-off is that soil needs its own rhythm — water when the top few centimetres feel dry, usually once a week or so — and it needs repotting every year or two as the plant grows top-heavy.

So which should you choose? If you like a ritual — changing the water every Sunday, rinsing the vase, topping it up — water works beautifully, and it looks better on a desk. If you would rather check the soil once a week and otherwise forget about it, soil is the safer bet. Use the comparison below to see the differences side by side.

## The water routine, step by step

If your lucky bamboo grows in water, the water itself is the whole growing medium — so its quality matters more than anything else in this guide. Fresh tap water often contains chlorine and fluoride, and this plant is sensitive to both. The leaves yellow from the tips inwards, which is the classic sign of a water problem, and it is the first thing to check whenever a water-grown plant looks unhappy.

The easy fix is to use filtered or distilled water, or to fill a jug with tap water and leave it uncovered for 24 hours before using it, which lets much of the chlorine escape. Note that resting the water helps with chlorine, not fluoride — if your tap water is high in fluoride and the leaves keep yellowing despite resting it, switch to filtered or distilled water.

Change the water every one to two weeks, and rinse the vase and pebbles each time. Stagnant water goes stale, grows algae, and can start to smell. Keep the roots submerged under roughly 2–5 cm of water at all times, topping up between changes if the level drops. If the stalks are tall and the vase is shallow, pebbles or marbles in the base keep the plant upright and stable.

Algae is the other water-grown headache. It thrives in light and stale water, so an opaque vase beats clear glass, direct sun makes it worse, and weekly water changes keep it in check. Rinse the pebbles and scrub the vase whenever you change the water.

## The soil routine — and switching between the two

Soil-grown lucky bamboo wants ordinary houseplant potting mix in a pot with drainage holes, kept evenly moist but never soggy. Water when the top 2–3 cm feels dry, usually once a week or so, and empty the saucer afterwards. Feed lightly in spring and summer with half-strength liquid fertiliser once a month at most, and none in autumn and winter. Repot every year or two, or when the plant becomes top-heavy.

Moving from water to soil is straightforward, and it is worth doing if a water-grown plant keeps struggling. Fill a pot with drainage holes with moist potting mix, plant the stalks so the roots are covered, and water it in well. Keep the soil consistently moist for the first few weeks while the water roots adapt — they are used to constant moisture and need time to adjust. After that, settle into the normal soil routine.

One direction only, though. Do not move a soil-grown plant back into water. Its roots are not built for constant submersion and will usually rot. The switch works from water to soil because soil is the more forgiving medium. The reverse is not true.

## Light, warmth, and the vase in the sun

Lucky bamboo prefers bright, indirect light — near a window, but out of direct sun. It tolerates low light well, which is why it does so well on desks and shelves, but in very dim spots growth slows and the leaves can look pale.

Direct sunlight is the one thing to avoid. Strong sun scorches the leaves, leaving bleached or brown patches, and it heats the water in a vase, which encourages algae — a double problem for water-grown plants that soil-grown ones mostly escape. A spot a metre or two from a sunny window, or beside an east-facing window, is ideal.

Temperature is simple: normal indoor temperatures, roughly 18–29°C. It dislikes cold draughts, so keep it away from draughty windows and air-conditioning vents in winter. Below about 10°C the plant suffers, and frost will kill it. Average indoor humidity is fine, and there is no need to mist it.

## Feeding: less than you think

Lucky bamboo needs very little fertiliser, and too much is worse than too little. For water-grown plants, a few drops of liquid houseplant fertiliser in the water every two to three months is enough. For soil-grown plants, a half-strength feed once a month in spring and summer is plenty, and none in autumn and winter.

Overfeeding shows up as yellow leaves, brown leaf tips, and in water-grown plants, a build-up of salts that can damage the roots. If you suspect overfeeding, change the water — or flush the soil with plain water — and skip feeding for a few months. The plant will recover. It just needs time and clean water.

## Pruning, shaping, and the rotting stalk

Lucky bamboo grows from the top of each stalk, and you can prune it to control its shape. Cut a stalk just above a node — the ring where a leaf grows — and new shoots will sprout from the node below the cut. The piece you cut off can be rooted in water: place it in a jar, keep the bottom node submerged, and roots appear within a few weeks.

Remove yellow or dead leaves promptly. And if a whole stalk turns yellow and soft, remove it straight away. A rotting stalk can spoil the water for the healthy ones sharing its vase, and in soil it can spread rot to its neighbours. If the rot is only at the bottom, you can try cutting the stalk well above the soft part and rooting the healthy top in fresh water — but if the whole stalk is soft, it is finished.

## When stalks go yellow

**Leaves turning yellow.** The most common complaint, and the cause is usually the water — chlorine or fluoride, stale water, or overfeeding. Switch to filtered or rested water, change it more often, and stop feeding for a while. Yellow leaves will not turn green again, so trim them off once you have fixed the cause.

**Stalk turning yellow and soft.** A soft, yellow stalk is rotting, usually from bacteria in stale water or from a damaged stalk. Remove it from the arrangement immediately, as above.

**Algae growing in the vase.** Algae thrives in light and stale water. Use an opaque vase rather than clear glass, keep the plant out of direct sun, and change the water weekly. Rinse the pebbles and scrub the vase when you change the water.

**Brown leaf tips.** Usually water quality, overfeeding, or very dry air. Try filtered water, cut back on fertiliser, and make sure the plant is not sitting next to a heater or air-conditioning vent.

## Questions people ask

### How often should I change the water for lucky bamboo?

Every one to two weeks. Rinse the vase and pebbles at the same time. If the water looks cloudy, smells, or has algae, change it more often. Between changes, top the water up so the roots stay covered.

### Can I use tap water for lucky bamboo?

You can, but leave it in an open jug for 24 hours first so the chlorine can escape. If your tap water is high in fluoride, or the leaves keep yellowing despite resting the water, switch to filtered or distilled water.

### Why are my lucky bamboo leaves turning yellow?

The most likely cause is the water — chlorine, fluoride, stale water, or too much fertiliser. Change to clean, rested water, change it regularly, and stop feeding for a few months. Yellow leaves will not recover, so remove them once the cause is fixed.

### Can lucky bamboo grow in soil?

Yes, and many growers find soil-grown plants are sturdier and live longer. Plant in ordinary potting mix in a pot with drainage, keep the soil evenly moist, and feed lightly in the growing season.

### Is lucky bamboo really bamboo?

No. It is a dracaena, a tropical plant related to other popular houseplants. The name comes from its bamboo-like stalks and its association with good luck in feng shui, not from any botanical relationship with true bamboo.
