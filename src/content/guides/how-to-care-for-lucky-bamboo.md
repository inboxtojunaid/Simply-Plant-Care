Lucky bamboo is one of the easiest indoor plants you can own — and one of the most misunderstood. Despite the name, it is not bamboo at all. It is a type of dracaena, a tropical plant that is usually sold as bare stalks in a vase of water, sometimes trained into curls, braids, or tiered shapes. It needs no soil, very little light, and almost no attention — which is why it ends up on desks and shelves everywhere.

The care is simple once you know the two things that matter most: the water has to be right, and the light has to be gentle. Get those two right and a lucky bamboo can live for years. Get them wrong and the leaves yellow, the stalks go soft, and the plant declines fast. This guide covers both water-grown and soil-grown plants, so whichever kind you have, you will know what to do.

## At a glance

| Need | What lucky bamboo wants |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light; tolerates low light |
| Water | Clean water, changed regularly; or moist (never soggy) soil |
| Water type | Filtered, distilled, or tap water left out for 24 hours |
| Container | Vase with pebbles for support, or a pot with drainage |
| Temperature | 18–29°C (65–85°F); keep away from cold draughts |
| Humidity | Average indoor humidity is fine |
| Feeding | Very light feeding, a few times a year at most |
| Difficulty | Easy — a good first plant |

## Water vs soil: which should you choose?

Most lucky bamboo is sold growing in water, and it can live that way happily for years. But it can also be planted in soil, and many growers find soil-grown plants are sturdier and live longer. ## Light

Lucky bamboo prefers bright, indirect light — near a window, but out of direct sun. It tolerates low light well, which is why it does so well on desks and shelves, but in very dim spots growth slows and the leaves can look pale.

Direct sunlight is the one thing to avoid. Strong sun scorches the leaves, leaving bleached or brown patches, and heats the water in a vase, which encourages algae. A spot a metre or two from a sunny window, or beside an east-facing window, is ideal.

## Water: the detail that matters most

If your lucky bamboo grows in water, the water itself is the whole growing medium — so its quality matters. Fresh tap water often contains chlorine and fluoride, and lucky bamboo is sensitive to both. The leaves yellow from the tips inwards, which is the classic sign of a water problem.

The easy fix is to use filtered or distilled water, or to fill a jug with tap water and leave it uncovered for 24 hours before using it, which lets much of the chlorine escape. Change the water every one to two weeks, and rinse the vase and pebbles each time. Stagnant water goes stale, grows algae, and can start to smell — and the roots suffer first.

Keep the roots submerged under roughly 2–5 cm of water at all times, topping up between changes if the level drops. If the stalks are tall and the vase is shallow, pebbles or marbles in the base keep the plant upright and stable.

## Moving from water to soil

If a water-grown plant is struggling, or you simply want a sturdier plant, you can move it into soil. Fill a pot with drainage holes with moist potting mix, plant the stalks so the roots are covered, and water it in well. Keep the soil consistently moist for the first few weeks while the water roots adapt — they are used to constant moisture and need time to adjust. After that, settle into the normal soil routine: water when the top few centimetres feel dry.

Do not move a soil-grown plant back into water — its roots are not built for constant submersion and will usually rot.

## Temperature and humidity

Lucky bamboo likes normal indoor temperatures, roughly 18–29°C. It dislikes cold draughts, so keep it away from draughty windows and air-conditioning vents in winter. Below about 10°C the plant suffers, and frost will kill it.

Average indoor humidity is fine, and there is no need to mist it.

## Feeding

Lucky bamboo needs very little fertiliser, and too much is worse than too little. For water-grown plants, a few drops of liquid houseplant fertiliser in the water every two to three months is enough. For soil-grown plants, a half-strength feed once a month in spring and summer is plenty, and none in autumn and winter.

Overfeeding shows up as yellow leaves, brown leaf tips, and in water-grown plants, a build-up of salts that can damage the roots. If you suspect overfeeding, change the water (or flush the soil with plain water) and skip feeding for a few months.

## Pruning and shaping

Lucky bamboo grows from the top of each stalk, and you can prune it to control its shape. Cut a stalk just above a node — the ring where a leaf grows — and new shoots will sprout from the node below the cut. The piece you cut off can be rooted in water: place it in a jar, keep the bottom node submerged, and roots appear within a few weeks.

Remove yellow or dead leaves promptly. If a whole stalk turns yellow and soft, remove it straight away — a rotting stalk can affect the healthy ones sharing its water.

## Common mistakes

- **Using fresh tap water.** Chlorine and fluoride yellow the leaves. Use filtered water or water left out for 24 hours.
- **Never changing the water.** Stale water grows algae and bacteria. Change it every one to two weeks.
- **Direct sun.** It scorches the leaves and heats the vase. Bright, indirect light is what it wants.
- **Letting the roots dry out.** In a vase, the roots must stay submerged. Top the water up between changes.
- **Overfeeding.** Lucky bamboo needs very little fertiliser. Too much burns the leaves and roots.
- **Leaving a rotting stalk in the vase.** One soft, yellow stalk can spoil the water for the rest. Remove it promptly.

## Troubleshooting

### Leaves turning yellow

Yellow leaves are the most common complaint, and the cause is usually the water — chlorine or fluoride, stale water, or overfeeding. Switch to filtered or rested water, change it more often, and stop feeding for a while. Yellow leaves will not turn green again, so trim them off once you have fixed the cause.

### Stalk turning yellow and soft

A soft, yellow stalk is rotting, usually from bacteria in stale water or from a damaged stalk. Remove it from the arrangement immediately. If the rot is only at the bottom, you can try cutting the stalk well above the soft part and rooting the healthy top in fresh water — but if the whole stalk is soft, it is finished.

### Algae growing in the vase

Algae thrives in light and stale water. Use an opaque vase rather than clear glass, keep the plant out of direct sun, and change the water weekly. Rinse the pebbles and scrub the vase when you change the water.

### Brown leaf tips

Brown tips usually point to water quality, overfeeding, or very dry air. Try filtered water, cut back on fertiliser, and make sure the plant is not sitting next to a heater or air-conditioning vent.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I change the water for lucky bamboo?

Every one to two weeks. Rinse the vase and pebbles at the same time. If the water looks cloudy, smells, or has algae, change it more often. Between changes, top the water up so the roots stay covered.

### Can I use tap water for lucky bamboo?

You can, but leave it in an open jug for 24 hours first so the chlorine can escape. If your tap water is high in fluoride, or the leaves keep yellowing despite resting the water, switch to filtered or distilled water.

### Why are my lucky bamboo leaves turning yellow?

The most likely cause is the water — chlorine, fluoride, stale water, or too much fertiliser. Change to clean, rested water, change it regularly, and stop feeding for a few months. Yellow leaves will not recover, so remove them once the cause is fixed.

### Can lucky bamboo grow in soil?

Yes, and many growers find soil-grown plants are sturdier and live longer. Plant in ordinary potting mix in a pot with drainage, keep the soil evenly moist, and feed lightly in the growing season.

### Is lucky bamboo really bamboo?

No. It is a dracaena, a tropical plant related to other popular houseplants. The name comes from its bamboo-like stalks and its association with good luck in feng shui, not from any botanical relationship with true bamboo.

## Conclusion

Lucky bamboo is easy to keep, but it is not a plant you can completely ignore. The whole routine is clean water changed regularly, gentle indirect light, and a very light touch with fertiliser.
