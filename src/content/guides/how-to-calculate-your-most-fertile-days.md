Type "most fertile days" into a search box and you will get advice about people, not plants. This guide is the plant version: the weeks of the year when your houseplants are growing fast, hungry, and ready to make good use of a feed — and how to work out when those weeks fall for the plants you actually own.

Every plant has a fertile window. It is not a single day circled on a calendar. It is a stretch of the year when light is strong, nights are warm, and new leaves are coming thick and fast. Feed inside that window and the plant puts the food to work. Feed outside it and the food sits in the soil, unused, building up salts that burn roots. The whole skill is knowing which weeks are which.

## What a plant's fertile days actually are

A plant is fertile, in the plainest sense, when it is building new tissue. New leaves unfurling, stems lengthening, roots pushing into fresh soil, flowers forming. All of that takes nutrients, and the plant takes them up fastest while it is growing. When growth slows, uptake slows with it — the roots do not keep drinking at summer speed just because the bottle says to feed every two weeks.

That is why the calendar on the fertiliser bottle is only a rough guide. "Feed every two weeks in spring and summer" assumes your spring looks like the one on the label. Your spring might be a north-facing flat in a cold April, or a bright conservatory where the monstera never really stopped. The plant's own growth tells you more than the label does, and the calculation in this guide starts from that growth.

Think of it as a window with soft edges. It opens gradually as days lengthen and closes gradually as they shorten. There is no morning when a plant wakes up fertile and no evening when it switches off. What you are really calculating is the middle of the window — the weeks when feeding pays best — and the shoulders on either side, when a light feed is fine but a heavy one is wasted.

## Three signals your plant is entering its hungry weeks

You do not need a light meter or a thermometer to spot the window opening. Three everyday signals do the job, and when two of them show up together, the fertile days have started.

First, longer days. You notice it before the plant does, in a way — the room stays light past dinner, the morning sun reaches further across the floor. Plants read day length the way we read a clock. Longer days mean more hours of photosynthesis, which means more growth, which means more hunger. If the light in your plant's spot has clearly stretched since February, that is signal one.

Second, warmer nights. Daytime warmth matters, but nights are the tell. A plant that spent winter in a cool room starts growing in earnest once nights stop feeling cold to your hand. If you have been keeping a plant in a chilly spare room, carry it back to its warm spot and watch what happens over the next fortnight — new growth is often the first thing you see.

Third, new growth itself. This is the only signal that cannot be argued with. A pothos pushing out a leaf every week, a snake plant sending up a new spear, a basil plant doubling in size — that plant is in its fertile days right now, whatever the calendar says. Slow, pale, or absent new growth means the window is still shut, or closing. Our guide on [how to take care of plants](/guides/how-do-you-take-care-of-plants/) covers what healthy new growth looks like across the common houseplants, if you are not sure what you are looking at.

## Counting back from your last feed

Here is the calculation itself, and it works backwards. Take your plant's feeding interval — the gap between feeds while it is growing — and count back from today to your last feed. That tells you whether you are on track, overdue, or feeding too often.

Say you have a pothos on a four-week cycle and your last feed was 5 April. Count forward in fours: 3 May, 31 May, 28 June, 26 July, 23 August, 20 September. That is your feeding line for the year, and it stops in September because the window closes in autumn. Now count back from today instead. If today is 12 May and your last feed was 5 April, you are a week overdue — the 3 May feed slipped past, and the plant has been growing hungry for nine days. If today is 12 May and you fed on 3 May, you are exactly on track, and the next one lands on 31 May.

The interval is the part people get wrong, because it changes with the plant. A heavy feeder on a two-week cycle — a monstera, a tomato, a rose — has a line with twice as many dates on it, and missing one matters more. A light feeder on an eight-week cycle — a snake plant, a ZZ plant, a cactus — has a line with only three or four dates in the whole year, and feeding it on the pothos schedule would be four times too much. The gap belongs to the plant, not to your routine, and writing the dates down is the only way to keep three different plants on three different cycles without guessing.

Try it with one plant first. Pick the one you feed most often, work out its interval, and write its dates from March to September on a card. Stick the card to the pot. You have just built a feeding calendar that fits that plant, and it took five minutes.

## The shape of a feeding year, month by month

Most houseplants in the northern hemisphere follow the same broad shape, and it helps to see the whole year at once before you zoom in on your own plants.

January and February are the quiet months. Days are short, nights are cold, and most houseplants are barely growing. Feeding now does almost nothing — the roots are not taking up much, and the salts stay in the soil. A few winter-growing plants are the exception, but for the ordinary houseplant, these are rest months.

March is when the window starts to open. Days lengthen noticeably, and the first new leaves appear on the fast growers. Start feeding the heavy feeders now, at half strength for the first feed or two, and let the moderate feeders wait until you see their new growth. April and May are the ramp-up: light is strong, nights are warmer, and most plants are growing properly. This is when the feeding line you calculated above starts to matter, because missing a feed in May costs the plant more than missing one in February ever could.

June, July, and August are the peak — the heart of the fertile days. Long days, warm nights, fast growth. Heavy feeders are at their hungriest here, and this is the only time of year when feeding every two weeks is genuinely what the plant wants. September is the shoulder: growth is still going, but the days are shortening and the plant is starting to slow. Keep feeding, but watch the new growth — when it thins out, the window is closing.

October is the last call for most plants. One final light feed early in the month, then stop. November and December are rest again. The plant is not being neglected; it is doing what it evolved to do, which is to sit out the dark months and wait for the light to come back.

## Reading light and temperature like a plant does

Plants do not own calendars, so they read the season from light and warmth, and you can learn to read it the same way. The cues are plain once you know to look for them.

Light first. Stand where your plant stands at midday and look at the shadow your hand casts on the wall. A sharp, dark shadow means strong light; a soft, pale one means weak light. Watch that shadow across the months. When it sharpens in spring and stays sharp through the afternoon, the plant has the light it needs to grow fast. When it fades in autumn to a faint smudge, growth is slowing whether you feed or not. A plant that moved to a brighter spot in March — closer to the window, or out of a dark corner — may open its fertile window weeks earlier than the same plant left in the shade.

Temperature second. Houseplants mostly grow fastest when days are warm and nights stay mild, and they slow down when nights turn cold. You can feel this without a thermometer: if you would want a jumper in the room at night, most tropical houseplants would too. A cold windowsill in February is not a feeding window, even if the days are getting longer — the light is saying go and the temperature is saying wait, and the plant listens to both.

Put the two cues together and you get a better answer than either gives alone. Bright light plus warm nights means the window is wide open. Bright light plus cold nights means the plant is waking up but not hungry yet — wait for the nights. Dim light plus warm nights means the plant wants to grow but cannot, and feeding it will not fix a light problem. That last case is the one that catches people out: food is not a substitute for light, and a plant fed in a dark corner just ends up with salty soil and the same pale leaves.

## Heavy feeders, light feeders, and the months in between

Not every plant's fertile window is the same length. Heavy feeders have a long window and a big appetite inside it; light feeders have a short window and a small one. Knowing which kind you own changes the calculation more than anything else.

Heavy feeders — monstera, bird of paradise, tomatoes, roses, most herbs you harvest hard — wake up early, often in March, and stay hungry into September. Their interval is short, about two weeks, and they notice a missed feed. If you grow [basil in pots](/guides/how-to-grow-basil-in-pots/), you know this already: a basil plant in June eats like it is making up for lost time, and it is, because every leaf you pick is growth it has to replace.

Moderate feeders — pothos, peace lilies, spider plants, most of the ordinary houseplants — open their window a little later, around April, and close it a little earlier, around September. Their interval is about four weeks. They are forgiving of a missed feed and forgiving of a late start, which is why they are the plants most people learn on.

Light feeders — snake plants, ZZ plants, cacti and [succulents](/guides/how-do-you-take-care-of-succulents/), rosemary, lavender — have the shortest window of all. They may only want three or four feeds in the whole year, spaced eight weeks apart, at quarter or half strength. Their fertile days are real but brief, and the biggest mistake with them is feeding on a moderate plant's schedule. A snake plant fed monthly all summer does not grow faster. It grows saltier soil.

The window also shifts with the plant's age and size. A young plant in a small pot, growing fast, is hungrier than a big old plant in the same spot. A plant you just repotted has fresh soil full of nutrients and does not need feeding for weeks — never fertilise a newly repotted plant, or a dry, stressed, or dormant one. Food helps a plant that is already growing. It does not rescue one that is not.

## The winter pause — and what feeding then actually does

The hardest part of the calculation is the part where the answer is nothing. Winter is the pause, and the pause is not neglect. It is the plant's year working as it should.

When light is short and nights are cold, growth slows to a crawl. The roots take up very little water and almost no food. A feed given in December does not wait politely in the soil until spring. It dissolves into salts that sit around the roots, pulling water out of them instead of in. The signs show up weeks later: brown leaf tips, a white crust on the soil surface, leaves that look burnt at the edges. People see those signs in February and reach for more feed, which is the opposite of what the plant needs — the fix is plain water, flushing the soil through, and then leaving it alone until growth restarts.

There is a second cost to winter feeding, quieter than the first. A plant pushed to grow in weak winter light makes soft, pale, stretched growth — long stems, small leaves, wide gaps between them. That growth is weak because the light cannot support it, and it stays weak. The plant spends spring repairing it instead of growing well. A plant left to rest makes no such growth, and starts spring with its strength intact.

So the winter rule is simple: when the plant stops growing, stop feeding. Resume when you see new growth in spring, not when the calendar says March. If you are unsure whether it has stopped, look at the newest leaf. If it has not changed in three weeks, the plant has told you its answer.

## A feeding log that takes thirty seconds

Everything in this guide gets easier if you write it down, and the log does not need to be a spreadsheet. A card tucked behind the pot, or a note on your phone, with three columns: plant, date fed, next feed due. That is the whole system.

Fill it in the moment you feed, while the watering can is still in your hand. Work out the next date from the interval — four weeks on for a pothos, two for a monstera, eight for a snake plant — and write it down before you put the can away. Then the calculation is done once, at feeding time, instead of being guessed at every time you look at the plant and wonder whether it is due.

The log also catches the mistakes that matter. A plant fed twice in three weeks because two people in the house both fed it. A plant that has not been fed since May because everyone assumed someone else did it. A feed given in November because the log showed a gap and nobody checked the season. The log is not about being strict. It is about knowing, instead of guessing, and guessing is where most feeding goes wrong.

Keep it to one line per plant, and keep it where you feed — by the sink, or on the shelf with the fertiliser bottle. If a system lives in a drawer, it does not get used. If it lives where the work happens, it takes thirty seconds, and thirty seconds is short enough to do every time.

A quick word on the rest of the routine, since feeding is only one part of keeping a plant alive. Watering, light, and repotting all shift with the same seasons — the plant that wants food in June also wants more water in June, and a bigger pot in spring rather than in December. ## Fertile days, asked and answered

### How do I know when my plant's fertile days start?

Look for the three signals together: days getting longer, nights staying warm, and new growth appearing. New growth is the surest sign — a plant pushing out fresh leaves is in its fertile window now, whatever the month. If there is no new growth, the window has not opened yet.

### How often should I feed in the fertile window?

It depends on the plant. Heavy feeders about every two weeks, moderate feeders about every four weeks, light feeders every six to eight weeks — all at half strength or weaker unless the label says otherwise, and only while the plant is actively growing. The interval belongs to the plant, not to your routine.

### Should I feed in winter?

For most houseplants, no. Growth slows in short days and cold nights, the roots take up almost nothing, and the feed builds up as salts that burn roots and brown leaf tips. Wait for new growth in spring, then start again. A few winter-growing plants are exceptions, but the ordinary houseplant rests in winter.

### What if I missed a feed in the middle of summer?

Feed now, at the usual strength, and carry on with the normal dates. Do not double up to catch up — a double dose does not make up for a missed one, it just adds salts the roots have to cope with. One missed feed in a season costs the plant very little.

### Can I feed a plant I just repotted?

Not yet. Fresh potting soil already holds nutrients, and damaged roots are easily burnt by fertiliser. Wait a few weeks until the plant is growing again in the new pot, then start feeding. The same goes for a dry, stressed, or dormant plant — food helps a plant that is already growing, it does not rescue one that is not.
