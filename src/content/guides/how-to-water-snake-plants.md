A snake plant is one of the easiest houseplants to water badly, not because watering it is complicated, but because it needs so little water that most people's normal routine is far too generous. The short answer is: water only when the soil has dried out completely, water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes, then let it drain fully and leave it alone until it is dry again.

That single habit — waiting for dry soil, then watering deeply — will prevent almost every watering problem a snake plant can have. The rest of this guide fills in the details: how often that usually means in your home, how much water to give, how the seasons change things, what kind of water to use, and how to read the plant when something has gone wrong.

## At a glance

| Factor | What a snake plant needs |
|---|---|
| How often | Every 2–6 weeks, only when soil is fully dry |
| How much | Enough to soak the soil until water drains out |
| Water type | Room-temperature tap water, left out overnight if heavily chlorinated |
| Soil | Very free-draining cactus or succulent mix |
| Pot | Must have drainage holes; empty the saucer after watering |
| Light effect | Brighter light dries soil faster; low light means water even less often |
| Difficulty | Easy, once you stop watering on a fixed weekly schedule |

## When to water a snake plant

Forget the calendar. A snake plant does not need water every Sunday, and in a cool, dim room it may not need water for six weeks or more. Its thick, fleshy leaves store water, so it is built to wait out dry spells. The trigger for watering is the soil, not the date.

Check the soil with your finger, pushed two to three inches down near the edge of the pot, or with a wooden skewer left in the soil for a minute. If it comes out damp or soil sticks to it, wait. If it is dry all the way down and the pot feels noticeably lighter than it did after watering, it is time. In a small pot in a bright, warm room that may be every two weeks in summer; in a large pot in a cool, low-light corner it may be every six to eight weeks in winter.

## How to water a snake plant, step by step

1. **Check first.** Push a finger or skewer into the soil. Only water if it is dry through most of the pot.
2. **Use room-temperature water.** Cold water can shock the roots; very hot water can damage them. A jug left out overnight also lets some chlorine dissipate.
3. **Water the soil, not the leaves.** Pour slowly and evenly around the base so the whole root ball gets wet. Avoid pouring water into the centre rosette, where the leaves meet — water sitting there can cause rot.
4. **Water until it drains.** Keep pouring until water runs freely from the drainage holes. This flushes out built-up salts and makes sure the lower roots are reached.
5. **Let it drain completely.** Leave the pot in the sink or on a draining rack for 15–20 minutes, then empty the saucer or cache pot. Never let the plant sit in standing water.
6. **Wait again.** Do not water again until the soil is dry. That waiting period is the most important part of the routine.

## Seasonal changes

Snake plants grow most actively in spring and summer, when longer days and warmer temperatures dry the soil faster and the plant uses more water. In those months, check the soil every two weeks and expect to water roughly every two to four weeks in a typical home.

In autumn and winter, growth slows or stops, light is weaker and rooms are cooler, so the soil stays wet much longer. Check monthly and expect to water every four to eight weeks — sometimes less. This is the season when most snake plants are killed by overwatering, because owners keep up a summer routine while the plant has stopped using the water. If your home is heated and very dry in winter, the soil may still dry out, so keep checking rather than assuming; the finger test beats the season every time.

## Signs of overwatering

Overwatering is the most common snake plant problem, and it usually shows up gradually:

- Leaves turning yellow, starting at the base
- Leaves feeling soft, mushy or translucent at the bottom
- Leaves leaning over or collapsing at the soil line
- Soil that smells sour or musty, or stays wet for weeks
- A mushy, brown base — the classic sign of root rot

If you catch it early, stop watering, move the plant to brighter light and better air circulation, and let the soil dry out completely. If the base is already mushy, remove the plant from the pot, cut away any soft, brown roots and leaves with clean scissors, let the healthy parts dry for a day, and repot in fresh, dry, free-draining mix. Do not water the repotted plant for at least a week.

## Common mistakes

- **Watering on a fixed weekly schedule.** The plant's needs change with light, season and pot size. A schedule ignores all of that.
- **Giving little sips.** Small, frequent waterings wet only the surface and leave the lower roots dry while salts accumulate.
- **Leaving water in the saucer.** Standing water keeps the bottom roots wet and invites rot.
- **Pouring water into the leaf rosette.** Water trapped where the leaves meet can rot the centre of the plant.
- **Watering a plant in a pot with no drainage.** Without an escape route, every watering adds to a puddle at the bottom.
- **Watering more to "help" a sick plant.** A yellowing, mushy snake plant is almost always overwatered already. More water makes it worse, not better.

## Troubleshooting

### My snake plant's leaves are yellow and soft at the base

This is usually overwatering or root rot. Stop watering immediately, check the soil — if it is wet, take the plant out of the pot and inspect the roots. Cut away anything soft and brown, repot in dry, free-draining mix, and do not water for a week or two.

### The leaf tips are brown and dry

This can be underwatering, but it can also be salt build-up from tap water or fertiliser. Check the soil moisture first: if it is bone dry, water thoroughly; if it is moist, flush the soil with a generous watering and let it drain, and trim the brown tips with clean scissors, cutting just inside the brown edge so a thin brown line remains and the cut looks natural.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I water a snake plant indoors?

Every two to six weeks is typical, but the real answer is whenever the soil has dried out completely. Check with your finger every couple of weeks in summer and every month in winter, and water only when the soil is dry.

### Can I water a snake plant once a week?

Almost certainly not. Weekly watering keeps the soil wet and is the fastest way to cause root rot. Snake plants store water in their leaves and prefer to dry out between waterings.

### Is it better to water a snake plant from the top or the bottom?

Either works. Top watering is simplest and flushes salts out; bottom watering is handy for very dry soil or for keeping the rosettes dry. Whichever you choose, let the pot drain fully afterwards and wait until the soil is dry before watering again.

### What happens if I forget to water my snake plant for two months?

It will probably be fine. A healthy snake plant can go for weeks without water, and may only show wrinkled leaves or dry tips. Give it a thorough soak, let it drain, and return to checking the soil regularly.

### How do I know if my snake plant has root rot from overwatering?

The base of the leaves feels soft and mushy, leaves may fall over at the soil line, and the soil smells sour. Take the plant out of the pot: healthy roots are firm and pale, rotted roots are brown, soft and stringy. Cut away the rot, repot in dry mix, and hold off watering.

## Conclusion

Watering a snake plant is less about doing something and more about waiting well. Check the soil, water thoroughly when it is dry, let the pot drain, and then leave the plant alone. Give it a pot with drainage holes and a free-draining mix, ease off in winter, and keep water out of the leaf rosettes. Do that, and watering stops being the thing that kills your snake plant and becomes the easiest part of looking after it.
