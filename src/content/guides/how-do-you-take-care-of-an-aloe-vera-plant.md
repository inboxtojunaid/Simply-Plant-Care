You take care of an aloe vera plant by giving it bright light, a very free-draining soil, and water only when the soil has dried out completely. Aloe is a succulent: its thick, fleshy leaves store water, which means it survives drought easily and dies quickly from too much water. If you remember one thing about aloe, make it this — underwatering is a mistake you can fix in a day; overwatering can kill the plant in weeks.

Aloe vera is one of the easiest houseplants to keep alive once you understand that. It wants sun, it wants to be left alone between waterings, and it wants a pot that drains. People who struggle with aloe almost always struggle because they water it like an ordinary houseplant.

## At a glance

| Need | What aloe vera wants |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright light, several hours of direct sun if possible |
| Water | Water deeply, then let the soil dry out completely before watering again |
| Soil | Very free-draining cactus or succulent mix; add extra perlite if needed |
| Pot | A terracotta pot with drainage holes is ideal |
| Temperature | 15–27 °C; keep above 10 °C, never frost |
| Humidity | Normal indoor humidity is fine; no misting needed |
| Feeding | Light feed once or twice in spring and summer, or none at all |
| Difficulty | Easy — the main risk is overwatering |

## Light: as much sun as you can give it

Aloe wants bright light and tolerates direct sun better than most houseplants. A south- or west-facing windowsill is ideal. Indoors, the brightest spot you have is the right spot. If the plant does not get enough light, the leaves stretch, turn pale, and flop outwards instead of growing upright in a tight rosette.

One warning: an aloe that has spent months in a dim spot can sunburn if it is moved suddenly into strong direct sun. Move it gradually — a week in bright indirect light, then into direct sun — so it can adjust. A sunburned aloe gets brown or orange patches on the leaves, but it will recover if moved back into gentler light.

## Watering: soak, then dry out fully

Aloe watering is the opposite of the routine for most houseplants. Instead of keeping the soil lightly moist, you soak the soil thoroughly and then leave it alone until it is completely dry all the way through the pot — not just the top few centimetres. In summer that usually means watering every two to three weeks; in winter, once a month or even less.

The finger test still works, but push your finger deep, or lift the pot — a dry pot is noticeably lighter. Better still, use a wooden skewer pushed to the bottom of the pot: if it comes out damp, wait. When you do water, water until it runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer. Aloe must never sit in water.

## Soil and potting

Aloe needs soil that drains fast. A ready-made cactus or succulent mix is the easiest choice. Ordinary houseplant compost holds too much water on its own — if you only have that, mix in roughly a third perlite or coarse grit to open it up. The test is simple: when you water, the water should drain through within seconds, not sit on the surface.

Terracotta pots suit aloe well because the clay breathes and the soil dries faster. Whatever pot you use, it must have drainage holes. Aloe should be repotted every two to three years in spring, going up one pot size. Aloe produces offsets — small "pups" around the base — which can be separated and potted on their own once they have a few leaves and some roots of their own.

## Feeding and general care

Aloe is a light feeder. One or two feeds of diluted cactus fertiliser in spring and summer are plenty, and many growers feed nothing at all. Overfeeding produces soft, weak growth, so if in doubt, skip it.

Aloe does not need misting or extra humidity — normal indoor air is fine, and wet leaves can invite rot. Wipe the leaves occasionally with a damp cloth to keep dust off. In summer, aloe can go outdoors into a sunny, sheltered spot, but bring it back inside well before the first frost, and move it gradually so it does not sunburn.

## Using the leaves

Aloe vera gel is widely used on skin, and the thick leaves are the source. If you want to use a leaf, cut a thick outer leaf close to the base with a clean knife, let the yellow sap drain off, and use the clear gel inside. Only ever take a few outer leaves at a time — the plant needs its leaves to live. Never take leaves from a young plant or a plant that is already stressed.

## Common mistakes

**Watering little and often.** Small, frequent waterings keep the top of the soil damp while the roots stay dry, or worse, keep the whole pot constantly damp. Water deeply, then wait until it is fully dry.

**No drainage hole.** Aloe in a pot without drainage is almost guaranteed to rot eventually. Drill a hole or change the pot.

**Dense compost.** Ordinary potting soil holds too much water for a succulent. Use a cactus mix or add perlite.

**Too little light.** Aloe in a dark corner stretches and flops. Give it the brightest spot you have.

**Cold and frost.** Aloe cannot tolerate frost, and even a cold windowsill in winter can damage it. Keep it above 10 °C.

**Misting.** Aloe does not want humidity or wet leaves. Skip the spray bottle.

## Troubleshooting

**Mushy, translucent leaves at the base:** overwatering and likely root rot. Stop watering, check the roots, trim away anything black and mushy, and repot in dry mix.

**Thin, curled leaves:** the plant is thirsty. Water thoroughly and the leaves should plump up within a day or two.

**Brown leaf tips:** usually sunburn, cold, or overfeeding. Move the plant slightly out of the harshest sun or away from a cold window, and ease off the fertiliser.

**Leaves turning red or orange:** a stress response, usually too much direct sun or too little water. It is not necessarily harmful, but move the plant to gentler light and water if the soil is dry.

**Plant leaning or flopping:** not enough light, or the pot is too small for the plant's weight. Move it brighter and consider a heavier terracotta pot.

**No pups, slow growth:** usually too little light or the plant is rootbound. More sun and a repot in spring usually restart growth.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I water aloe vera?

Only when the soil is completely dry all the way through the pot — roughly every two to three weeks in summer and once a month or less in winter. Always check the soil first: if it is still damp at depth, wait. Overwatering is the most common cause of aloe death.

### Why is my aloe vera turning brown?

Brown, soft leaves at the base usually mean overwatering or root rot. Dry brown tips can mean sunburn, cold, or underwatering. Work out which it is — soft and mushy points to too much water; thin and dry points to too little — and adjust from there.

### Can aloe vera live in low light?

It will survive in moderate light, but it will not thrive. Aloe needs bright light to keep its leaves thick and upright. In low light it stretches, pales, and flops. A sunny windowsill is the best indoor spot.

### Should I mist my aloe vera plant?

No. Aloe is a succulent from dry climates and does not need extra humidity. Misting wets the leaves and can encourage rot in the centre of the rosette. Normal indoor air is fine.

### How do I know if my aloe has root rot?

The signs are mushy, translucent leaves at the base, leaves falling off with a light touch, and soil that stays wet and may smell sour. To confirm, unpot the plant — healthy aloe roots are pale and firm, rotted roots are dark and mushy. Trim the rot away and repot in dry mix.

### Can I use the leaves from my aloe plant?

Yes — cut a thick outer leaf at the base with a clean knife, let the yellow sap drain, and use the clear gel inside. Take only a few outer leaves at a time, and only from a healthy, mature plant.

## Conclusion

Aloe vera is a plant that rewards neglect more than attention. Bright light, fast-draining soil, a pot with holes, and water only when the soil is fully dry — that is the whole routine. If your aloe is struggling, the answer is almost always less water, not more. Get the watering right and an aloe will grow steadily for years, produce pups, and give you thick leaves to use whenever you need them.
