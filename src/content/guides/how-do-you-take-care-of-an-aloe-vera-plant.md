A healthy aloe leaf feels like a firm water balloon. Squeeze one gently and it resists, then springs back. The rosette sits tight and upright, each leaf thick at the base and tapering to a soft point, the green a deep, even colour. That is the whole picture of a plant doing well.

Now picture the other one. Leaves thin and curling inward like a closing hand. Or worse — leaves going soft and glassy at the base, turning yellow from the bottom up, and coming away in your fingers when you brush past. Same plant, same species. The difference is almost always water, and the fix depends on which side of the line you're on.

## The mistake almost everyone makes first

Here's the uncomfortable bit: most aloe deaths are acts of kindness. The plant looks dry, the top of the soil looks dry, so you water it. A week later it still looks dry, so you water it again. The soil never dries out, the roots sit in damp compost, and rot sets in long before the leaves show it.

Aloe is a succulent. Those thick leaves are water tanks — the plant evolved in dry places where rain is rare and the soil drains in minutes. It handles drought the way a camel handles a desert: by not needing much, for a long time. What it cannot handle is wet feet. Underwatering is a mistake you fix in a day. Overwatering can kill the plant in weeks, and by the time the leaves turn mushy, the roots are often already gone.

So the rule to carry around is simple. When in doubt, wait. An aloe can go weeks without water. It cannot go days with rotting roots.

## Reading your plant: before and after

Before you change anything, look at the plant the way a mechanic looks at an engine — what's actually happening, not what you assume is happening.

A thirsty aloe looks deflated. Leaves thin out, curl inward, and feel soft in a papery way — like a balloon that's lost air but hasn't burst. The tips dry out and go brown. The soil has usually pulled away from the edges of the pot. The plant stays upright, though. It looks unhappy, not sick.

A drowned aloe looks different, and the difference matters. Leaves go soft and translucent at the base, almost jelly-like. They yellow from the bottom up. The soil stays wet for days and may smell sour or musty. Leaves fall off with the lightest touch. That is rot, and it needs the opposite response: stop watering entirely, not more of it.

If your plant matches the thirsty picture, a thorough watering should plump the leaves back up within a day or two. If it matches the drowned picture, take it out of the pot and look at the roots. Pale and firm is fine. Dark, soft, and mushy is rot — cut those roots away, let the plant dry for a day, and repot it in dry cactus mix. Then don't water for at least a week. Yes, a week. The plant will cope.

## Light: give it the sunniest spot you've got

Light is the other half of the before-and-after picture. An aloe in good light grows in a tight, upright rosette with thick leaves. An aloe in a dim room stretches, pales, and flops outward — the leaves reaching for a window that isn't bright enough. The plant isn't dying. It's just telling you where the sun is.

Put it on the brightest windowsill you have, ideally south- or west-facing. Aloe tolerates direct sun better than most houseplants, and a few hours of it each day is exactly what it wants. One caution, though: a plant that's spent months in a dim corner can sunburn if you throw it straight into strong sun. Brown or orange patches on the leaves are the sign. Move it gradually — a week in bright indirect light first, then into direct sun — and it will adjust. A sunburned aloe recovers once it's back in gentler light.

## Watering on a soak-and-dry rhythm

Forget "a little water every few days." That is the routine that kills aloes. The right rhythm is soak, then dry out completely, then soak again.

When you water, water properly — until it runs from the drainage holes, then tip the saucer out. Aloe must never sit in water. Then leave it alone until the soil is dry all the way through the pot, not just the top few centimetres. In summer that usually means every two or three weeks; in winter, once a month or even less.

How do you know it's dry at depth? Push a finger in deep, or lift the pot — a dry pot is noticeably lighter. A wooden skewer pushed to the bottom works well too: if it comes out damp, wait. And if the soil has gone so dry it's hydrophobic, with water running straight through the pot without soaking in, set the pot in a basin of water for twenty minutes and let it drink from below.

## Soil and the pot that saves you

Fast drainage is the whole game here. A ready-made cactus or succulent mix is the easiest choice. Ordinary houseplant compost holds too much water on its own — if that's all you have, mix in roughly a third perlite or coarse grit to open it up. The test is simple: when you water, it should drain through within seconds, not sit on the surface.

Terracotta suits aloe because the clay breathes and the soil dries faster. Whatever pot you choose, it must have drainage holes. No hole, no aloe — a plant in a sealed pot will rot eventually, however careful you are. Repot every two to three years in spring, going up one pot size. And keep an eye out for pups: small offsets around the base that can be separated and potted on their own once they have a few leaves and some roots.

Feeding? Barely. One or two light feeds of diluted cactus fertiliser in spring and summer are plenty, and many growers feed nothing at all. Too much fertiliser makes soft, weak growth. If you're unsure, skip it. Aloe doesn't need misting either — normal indoor air is fine, and wet leaves invite rot. A wipe with a damp cloth now and then keeps the dust off.

In summer it can go outside to a sunny, sheltered spot, but bring it back in well before the first frost, and harden it off gradually so it doesn't sunburn. Aloe can't take frost at all, and even a cold windowsill in winter can damage it. Keep it above 10 °C.

## Taking a leaf — and the pups

The gel inside the thick leaves is the reason many people grow aloe in the first place. If you want to use a leaf, cut a thick outer one close to the base with a clean knife, let the yellow sap drain off, and use the clear gel inside. Only take a few outer leaves at a time, and never from a young plant or one that's already stressed. The plant needs its leaves to live.

Pups are the other reward. Once a pup has a few leaves of its own and some roots, you can tease it away from the mother plant and pot it in the same fast-draining mix. Water it in lightly, then treat it like a small adult: sun, then dry soil, then water.

## What usually goes wrong

**Mushy, translucent leaves at the base.** Overwatering, and probably root rot. Stop watering, check the roots, trim away anything black and mushy, repot in dry mix.

**Thin, curled leaves.** The plant is thirsty. Water thoroughly — the leaves should plump up within a day or two.

**Brown leaf tips.** Usually sunburn, cold, or overfeeding. Move it slightly out of the harshest sun or away from a cold window, and ease off the fertiliser.

**Leaves turning red or orange.** A stress response, most often too much direct sun or too little water. It isn't necessarily harmful, but give the plant gentler light and water if the soil is dry.

**Plant leaning or flopping.** Not enough light, or a pot too small for the plant's weight. Move it brighter and consider a heavier terracotta pot.

**No pups, slow growth.** Usually too little light or a rootbound plant. More sun and a spring repot tend to restart growth.

## Questions people ask

### How often should I water aloe vera?

Only when the soil is completely dry all the way through the pot — roughly every two to three weeks in summer and once a month or less in winter. Check the soil first: if it's still damp at depth, wait. Overwatering is the most common cause of aloe death.

### Why is my aloe vera turning brown?

Brown, soft leaves at the base usually mean overwatering or root rot. Dry brown tips can mean sunburn, cold, or underwatering. Soft and mushy points to too much water; thin and dry points to too little. Work out which it is and adjust from there.

### Can aloe vera live in low light?

It will survive in moderate light, but it won't thrive. In low light it stretches, pales, and flops. A sunny windowsill is the best indoor spot for it.

### Should I mist my aloe vera plant?

No. Aloe is a succulent from dry climates and doesn't need extra humidity. Misting wets the leaves and can encourage rot in the centre of the rosette. Normal indoor air is fine.

### How do I know if my aloe has root rot?

The signs are mushy, translucent leaves at the base, leaves falling off with a light touch, and soil that stays wet and may smell sour. To confirm, unpot the plant — healthy aloe roots are pale and firm, rotted roots are dark and mushy. Trim the rot away and repot in dry mix.

### Can I use the leaves from my aloe plant?

Yes — cut a thick outer leaf at the base with a clean knife, let the yellow sap drain, and use the clear gel inside. Take only a few outer leaves at a time, and only from a healthy, mature plant.
