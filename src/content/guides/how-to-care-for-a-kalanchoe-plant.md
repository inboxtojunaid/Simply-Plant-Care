A kalanchoe in flower is hard to miss: clusters of small, bright blooms — red, pink, orange, yellow, or white — sitting on top of thick, glossy leaves, and they last for two to three months. Then the flowers fade, the plant goes quiet, and most people assume the show is over. It isn't. The show is on a timetable, and the timetable is the whole point of this plant.

Kalanchoe is a succulent that happens to flower. Treat it like one and it thrives. Treat it like a thirsty flowering plant and it rots. And if you want it to flower again — which is the part most people struggle with — it needs long, dark nights in autumn. That's the secret. Everything else is maintenance.

## The short version

Bright light, ideally a sunny windowsill. Water only when the soil has dried out through the pot — roughly every one to two weeks in the growing season, less in winter. Fast-draining soil, a pot with holes, a monthly half-strength feed in spring and summer. After flowering, cut the spent stems back. Then, in early autumn, give it 14 hours of uninterrupted darkness every night for about six weeks, and the buds will follow. That's the whole routine, and the rest of this guide is the detail behind it.

## Flowers first: what a blooming plant needs day to day

Kalanchoe needs more light than most houseplants. A sunny windowsill — south- or west-facing is ideal — suits it well, and it can take several hours of direct sun. In lower light it survives, but the stems stretch, the leaves grow sparse, and flowering is weak or absent. If your plant looks leggy with long gaps between leaves, it's asking for more light.

Watering follows succulent rules. Water only when the soil has dried out through the pot, water thoroughly, let the excess drain away completely, and never let the pot sit in water. Overwatering is the main way to kill a kalanchoe. Its thick leaves store water, so it copes with drought far better than with soggy soil. Soft, translucent lower leaves, a mushy stem base, and black spots on the leaves are the signs you've gone too far — stop watering, let the soil dry fully, and check the drainage holes aren't blocked.

The soil should be loose and fast-draining. A cactus or succulent potting mix is ideal, or ordinary potting mix with a generous handful of perlite or coarse grit mixed in. The pot must have drainage holes, and kalanchoe stays compact and prefers a snug pot, so repot only every two to three years, in spring, moving up one size.

Feeding is monthly from spring to early autumn, a balanced liquid fertiliser at half strength. Don't feed in winter, and don't overfeed — too much fertiliser produces soft, leggy growth and few flowers.

And deadhead. Cut each spent flower cluster back to just above a pair of leaves. It keeps the plant tidy and encourages it to put energy into new growth rather than seed. Once all the flowers on a stem have finished, cut the whole stem back.

## After the flowers fade: the prune that sets up next year

This is the step people skip, and it's the one that decides whether next year's plant is a bush or a stalk. After flowering, kalanchoe often grows tall and straggly. In spring, cut leggy stems back by up to half, just above a leaf joint, and the plant will branch out with fresh, bushy growth.

Don't feel bad about cutting it hard. The plant responds by branching, and the cuttings root easily in potting mix or water — so the pieces you prune off become new plants. A single kalanchoe, pruned once a year, can quietly become three or four.

## Summer: growing the plant that will bloom

Summer is the growing season, and it's doing quiet work. Give the plant bright light, water when the soil dries, and feed monthly at half strength. A healthy, well-fed plant makes far more buds than a starved one — the autumn treatment only works on a plant that's in good shape.

In summer a kalanchoe can go outdoors onto a sunny patio or balcony, which often encourages a strong flush of growth. Move it gradually into full sun outdoors so the leaves don't scorch, and bring it back inside before night temperatures drop below about 10 °C. It isn't frost-hardy, and a cold night will do more damage than a dry week ever could.

## Autumn: six weeks of long nights

Here's the part that matters. Kalanchoe is a short-day plant: it sets flower buds when nights are long and uninterrupted. In early autumn, begin giving it 14 hours of total darkness every night for about six weeks.

Practically, that means from around 6pm to 8am, the plant sits in a dark room, or under a box or a dark cloth. Be strict about it. Even a brief flash of light during the dark hours — a lamp switched on, a hallway light — can interrupt bud formation. During the day, keep the plant in its usual bright spot. The treatment is about the long night, not about reducing daytime light. Continue watering when the soil dries, but stop feeding once the treatment starts.

## Buds to blooms: the final stretch

After about six weeks, tiny flower buds should appear at the stem tips. Once you see them, stop the dark treatment and return the plant to normal day and night. The buds will open over the following weeks into a full flush of flowers — and that flush can last two to three months with good light and care.

That's the payoff for the whole timeline: prune in spring, grow through summer, dark nights in autumn, flowers in winter. The same plant can do it year after year. The calendar doesn't change, and neither does the plant's end of the bargain. All it asks is that you remember the autumn part.

## If it won't flower

**Leaves are soft and translucent at the base.** Overwatering. Let the soil dry out completely, remove any mushy leaves, and check the roots aren't black and rotten. If the stem base is mushy, take a healthy cutting and start again.

**The plant is tall and leggy with few leaves.** Not enough light. Move it to a brighter spot and prune the leggy stems back in spring to encourage branching.

**It grows well but never flowers.** Almost always a light or day-length problem. First, give it more light. Then, in autumn, run the six-week short-day treatment above. Without long, uninterrupted nights, a kalanchoe simply won't set buds — no matter how healthy it looks.

**Brown, crispy leaf edges.** Usually sun scorch after a sudden move into strong sun, or salt build-up from over-fertilising. Move it into sun gradually, and flush the soil with plain water if you've been feeding heavily.

**White powdery patches on the leaves.** Powdery mildew, encouraged by poor air circulation and damp leaves. Remove affected leaves, improve airflow, and avoid wetting the foliage when watering.

## Questions people ask

### How often should I water a kalanchoe?

Only when the soil has dried out through the pot — roughly every one to two weeks in the growing season, less in winter. Water thoroughly and let it drain completely.

### Why won't my kalanchoe flower again?

Kalanchoe needs long, dark nights to set flower buds. In autumn, give it about 14 hours of uninterrupted darkness each night for six weeks. Low light can also prevent flowering, so make sure it has a bright spot during the day.

### Can a kalanchoe live outside?

In summer, yes — it enjoys a sunny patio or balcony once night temperatures stay above about 10 °C. It is not frost-hardy, so it must come indoors before the cold arrives.

### Is kalanchoe toxic to pets?

Kalanchoe is considered toxic to cats and dogs if eaten, so keep it out of reach of pets that chew plants, and out of reach of small children.

### Should I cut off the dead flowers?

Yes. Cut each spent flower cluster back to just above a pair of leaves. This keeps the plant tidy and encourages new growth.
