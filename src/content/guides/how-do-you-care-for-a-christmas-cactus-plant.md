Look at the name and you picture a desert. Spines, sand, a plant that wants to be left alone in the sun. Wrong plant. This one grows in the treetops of the Brazilian rainforest, tucked into branch forks where rain drips through and light comes filtered through leaves. Flat green segments, no spines. It likes humidity, shade, and soil that stays lightly moist.

That one fact changes almost everything. Treat it like a desert cactus and it sulks: limp segments, no buds, tired by November. Treat it like the forest plant it is and it does what people buy it for — pink, red, or white flowers in late autumn and winter, just when most houseplants have gone quiet.

The short answer? Bright, indirect light. Water when the top soil dries, a little more while it buds. Cool nights and long, dark nights from late September. And once the buds appear, leave it exactly where it is.

## It's a cactus that hates the desert

Schlumbergera is a forest cactus. In the wild it grows as an epiphyte, perched on trees rather than rooted in desert sand. The air is damp, the light is dappled, and its roots sit in a thin layer of leaf litter that holds moisture but drains fast. No blazing sun, no bone-dry soil, no hot windowsill above a radiator.

That is why the care in our guide to [how to take care of a cactus indoors](/guides/how-to-take-care-of-a-cactus-indoors/) is almost the opposite of what this plant needs. Desert cacti store water in thick stems and wait out droughts. This one stores far less, and its flat segments go limp and wrinkled when it stays dry too long. If you keep [succulents](/guides/how-do-you-take-care-of-succulents/), think of this plant as the odd one out — the cousin that moved to the rainforest and never looked back.

Give it a loose, free-draining houseplant mix, with a handful of perlite or orchid bark stirred in so air reaches the roots. The pot must have drainage holes. Damp, not soggy, is the target. Repot only every two or three years, in spring, moving up one pot size. It flowers best when slightly snug.

## Don't move it once the buds appear

Here is the heartbreak of this plant. You see buds forming. You move the pot somewhere better so everyone can see it. Next morning the buds are on the table.

Bud drop is the plant's stress response. A sudden change — a new position, a cold draught, a hot radiator, a room swinging from warm to cold — and it sheds the buds it spent weeks making. It is not dying. It is cutting its losses.

So pick its flowering spot early, before the buds form, and then leave it alone. No rotating for a better photo. No carrying it into the living room for a party and back again. Keep it away from front doors, open windows in cold weather, and radiators. Hot, dry air dries the buds fast. A cold draught can make them drop overnight. A steady room, about 15–21°C by day, suits it best — and the cool end of that range at night is what helps it flower.

If buds do drop, put the plant back where it was happy, keep the watering steady, and wait. Next autumn, with the same spot from the start, it gets another chance.

## Cool nights and long darkness: how the flowers get switched on

Flowers don't appear because December arrives. They appear because the plant has had weeks of the right signals: cool nights and long, uninterrupted nights.

From late September or October, it needs two things at once. Night temperatures around 10–15°C. And 12–14 hours of darkness every night, for about six weeks. In a normal home the nights are already getting longer in autumn — but the dark must be truly dark. A lamp left on, a hallway light, a streetlight through thin curtains: any of those can weaken the signal.

That sounds fussy. In practice most people get there without a cupboard routine — a cool spare room with curtains drawn at night often does it. What matters is consistency: six weeks of long nights, not three good ones and four lit ones.

A [poinsettia](/guides/how-to-care-for-poinsettias-inside/) also needs long dark nights, but for longer — about eight weeks — and it colours its leaves rather than opening flowers. Same season, not the same method.

## Watering by the season, not by the calendar

Watering is where the desert instinct does the most damage. A fixed weekly schedule ignores the plant's year, and its year has three very different phases.

While it is budding and flowering — late autumn into winter — it drinks more. Keep the soil lightly moist. Check the top 2–3 cm with your finger every few days, and water when it feels dry there. Water thoroughly, let the excess drain away, and empty the saucer. Never let the pot sit in water.

After flowering finishes, ease off. The plant rests. Water less, just enough to keep the soil from drying out completely. Through spring and summer, while it grows new segments, water when the top soil dries — a middle ground. Use room-temperature water.

## Where to put it: light, warmth, and the draughts to avoid

Light is simple once you know the forest rule. Bright, indirect light. An east-facing window is ideal — gentle morning sun, shade the rest of the day. A north-facing window works too, though flowering may be lighter. A south- or west-facing window can work if the plant sits a little back from the glass, or behind a sheer curtain.

Direct sun is the problem. A few hours of harsh midday sun scorches the segments, turning them reddish and papery. A plant that has gone red is not dead — move it back into indirect light, water it, and new growth comes through green. The scorched segments stay marked, though, so prevention beats repair.

Warmth? Steady and moderate, about 15–21°C by day. Dry air above a radiator and cold air by a draughty door are both trouble. Humidity helps: a bathroom with a window, or a pebble tray of water under the pot, raises the moisture in the air.

Bright enough to read by, no sunbeams landing on the leaves at noon, no radiator underneath, no door opening onto cold air beside it. Find that place in September and the plant can stay there all winter. If you want a trailing plant for a dimmer corner instead, [pothos](/guides/how-to-care-for-pothos/) copes with far less light — this one does not, at least not if you want flowers.

## After the flowers: the quiet months

The flowers fade in late winter. The plant looks plain again — just green segments, no show. This is normal. Don't throw it away, and don't try to force a second flush.

Snip off the spent flowers where they meet the stem. Then let it rest. Water less, stop feeding, and keep it in its bright spot.

Feeding is light and seasonal. A balanced liquid feed at half strength, once a month from spring to early autumn, is plenty. Don't feed in winter, and don't feed a plant resting after flowering. Too much feed makes soft, leggy growth and few buds.

## The whole routine, boiled down

Bright indirect light, all year. Soil lightly moist while budding and flowering, drier after the flowers fade, middling through the growing season. Cool nights around 10–15°C and 12–14 hours of darkness every night for about six weeks from late September. A steady spot, away from radiators and draughts, that you do not move once buds appear. Feed monthly at half strength in spring and summer, never in winter. Repot every two or three years in spring. That is the whole job.

## When it sulks: buds, limp stems, and no flowers

**Buds form, then drop.** Usually a move, a draught, or a sudden temperature change after the buds appeared. Put it back in its steady spot and leave it alone.

**No buds at all.** Almost always the autumn signals were missing — nights too warm, or not dark enough for long enough. Next year, start the long nights in late September, keep the nights cool, and be strict about lamps after dark.

**Limp, wrinkled segments.** Thirst, most of the time. Water thoroughly and they should plump up within a day or two. If the soil is already wet and it still wilts, the roots may be damaged — let the soil dry out.

**Segments turning red or purple.** Too much direct sun, or sometimes cold stress. Move it into bright indirect light and keep the temperature steady.

**Mushy stem base.** Overwatering with poor drainage. Stop watering, let the soil dry, and check the pot drains. If the base is mushy, take a healthy segment as a cutting and start again.

## Christmas cactus, asked and answered

### How often should I water a Christmas cactus?

When the top 2–3 cm of soil feels dry — more often while it is budding and flowering, less after the flowers fade. Water thoroughly, let it drain, and never let it sit in water.

### Why did my Christmas cactus drop its buds?

A sudden change after the buds formed: moving the pot, a cold draught, or hot dry air from a radiator. Pick its flowering spot before the buds appear and leave it there.

### How do I get it to flower again next year?

From late September, give it cool nights around 10–15°C and 12–14 hours of darkness every night for about six weeks. Keep it in bright indirect light by day.

### Can a Christmas cactus live outside in summer?

In a sheltered, shady spot, yes — once the nights stay mild. Bring it back inside before nights turn cold, and move it gradually so the light change doesn't scorch it.

### Is a Christmas cactus safe around pets?

It is generally considered non-toxic to cats and dogs. Still, keep it out of reach of a pet that chews leaves, since eating a lot of any plant can upset a stomach.
