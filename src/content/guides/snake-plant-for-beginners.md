If you have never kept a houseplant alive, a snake plant is the best place to start. It tolerates low light, forgetful watering, dry air and a missed month of attention, and it still looks sculptural and deliberate sitting in a corner. You do not need a green thumb, special equipment or a complicated routine — you need a healthy plant, a pot with drainage, and the willingness to mostly leave it alone.

This guide is written for a true beginner: someone buying their very first plant. It covers why the snake plant is so forgiving, how to choose a good one at the shop, what to do in your first week, the four mistakes beginners make most often, and a simple routine you can follow from month one onwards.

## Why a snake plant is so forgiving

Most houseplants fail for beginners because they need a precise balance: enough water but not too much, enough light but not direct sun, enough humidity but not wet leaves. A snake plant has a wide margin for error on almost all of these.

Its thick, upright leaves are water-storage organs, like a succulent's. That means it can go weeks without water and barely notice, which suits a beginner who is still learning when to water — and, more importantly, when not to. It grows slowly, so it does not suddenly outgrow its pot or demand repotting. It is comfortable in the dry air of a heated home, it does not need misting, and it tolerates a dim corner that would weaken most other plants. It will grow faster and look its best in bright light, but it will survive in low light, which is more than can be said for most beginner plants.

## Buying your first snake plant

You do not need a rare variety. The common green snake plant, with tall upright leaves edged in yellow, is cheap, widely available and just as tough as any other. Smaller plants in 10–15 cm pots are ideal for a first purchase: they are affordable, easy to move, and you can see the whole plant clearly.

At the shop, check five things before you buy:

1. **Leaves firm and upright.** Squeeze a leaf gently — it should feel firm and fleshy, not soft or wrinkled. Leaves should stand up on their own, not flop over.
2. **A firm base.** The leaves should feel solid where they meet the soil. A wobbly or mushy base can mean rot has already started.
3. **No yellow or mushy leaves.** A little brown on a tip is cosmetic, but yellow, translucent or soft leaves are a warning sign.
4. **Clean leaves.** Look under the leaves for tiny insects or sticky residue. A healthy plant has clean, dust-free leaves.
5. **Dry-ish soil, not soaking wet.** If the soil is sopping and the pot is heavy with water, the shop has been overwatering it. Choose a plant whose soil is damp at most.

Do not worry about a perfect shape. Snake plants grow new leaves from the centre, and a slightly uneven plant will fill out over time. Do buy a pot with drainage holes if the plant does not already have one — or plan to repot it at home in the first week.

## Your first week: setting up

Your first week with a snake plant is mostly about placement and patience. Here is a simple sequence:

**Day 1 — Find its spot.** Put it near a window with bright, indirect light if you have one — a few feet back from a sunny window, or beside an east-facing window. If your only spot is a dim corner, that is fine too; it will grow more slowly, but it will live. Keep it away from radiators and cold draughts.

**Day 1 — Check the pot.** Lift the plant out of any decorative outer pot and check for drainage holes. If there are none, either drill some, use the outer pot only as a cover and take the plant out to water it, or repot into a pot with holes. This is the single most useful thing you can do in week one.

**End of week one — First soil check.** Push your finger two inches into the soil. If it is damp, wait. If it is dry, water thoroughly until water runs from the drainage holes, let it drain for 15 minutes, and empty the saucer. Then start the checklist below.

## The 4 mistakes beginners make

### 1. Watering every week

This is the number one killer. A snake plant watered weekly sits in wet soil, its roots rot, and the leaves turn yellow and mushy at the base. Water on the soil's schedule, not yours — only when it is dry.

### 2. Using a pot with no drainage

A beautiful pot without holes is a trap. Water collects at the bottom, out of sight, and rots the roots. Always use a pot with holes, or keep the plant in a nursery pot inside the decorative one.

### 3. Putting it in a dark cupboard or bathroom with no window

Snake plants tolerate low light, but "low light" still means some light. A windowless room is not low light — it is no light, and no plant can live there. It needs at least some natural light, or a grow light, to survive.

### 4. Watering the centre of the plant

Pouring water into the rosette, where the leaves join at the centre, lets water sit and rot the crown. Always water the soil around the base instead.

## Troubleshooting for beginners

### The leaves are turning yellow

Yellow, soft leaves at the base usually mean too much water. Stop watering, let the soil dry out completely, and check that the pot drains. If the base feels mushy, you may need to remove the rotted parts and repot in dry mix.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a snake plant really good for beginners?

Yes — it is one of the most forgiving houseplants you can buy. It tolerates low light, dry air and irregular watering, and it grows slowly, so it does not demand frequent repotting or pruning. Its main weakness is overwatering, which is easy to avoid once you know to wait for dry soil.

### How often should a beginner water a snake plant?

Only when the soil is completely dry, which is usually every two to six weeks depending on light, season and pot size. Check the soil with your finger before watering, and never water on a fixed weekly schedule.

### Where should I put my first snake plant?

In the brightest spot you have that does not get harsh, direct midday sun for hours — a few feet from a sunny window is ideal. It will also cope in a dimmer corner, but it will grow more slowly there. Avoid placing it right next to a radiator or in a cold draught.

### Do snake plants need direct sunlight?

No. They do best in bright, indirect light. A little gentle morning sun is fine, but strong direct sun can scorch the leaves, especially if the plant is not used to it. If you move a plant into a sunnier spot, do it gradually over a week or two.

### How big will my snake plant get?

It depends on the variety and the pot. Small varieties stay under 30 cm; common varieties can reach 60–120 cm tall over several years. Growth is slow, especially in low light, so you will not wake up to a giant plant.

## Conclusion

A snake plant is a beginner's plant precisely because it asks so little of you: a bright spot, a pot that drains, and water only when the soil is dry. Buy a healthy one, set it up well in the first week, and resist the urge to fuss over it. The beginners who lose snake plants are almost never the ones who neglect them — they are the ones who water them too often, in a pot with no drainage. Do less, check the soil, and your first plant will still be standing tall a year from now, probably with a new leaf or two to show for it.
